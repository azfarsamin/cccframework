## Abstract and keywords

Reassessing Prophetic Literacy and the Written Transmission of Revelation: A Linguistic and Textual Analysis of the Qurʾān

Abstract

This article reexamines two long-standing assumptions in Islamic tradition: that Prophet Muḥammad was illiterate and that the Qurʾān was first codified only under the caliph ʿUthmān. Through a pan-textual and morphological analysis (RYDING 2005; OWENS 2013; HOLES 2018), it argues that the Qurʾānic term ʾummī denotes “unscriptured”—one without prior revelation—rather than “unlettered” (Q 2:78). Internal linguistic evidence, including the reflexive verb iktatabahā (Q 25:5) contrasted with causative forms, together with references to an inscribed kitāb (Q 52:2–3), ṣuḥuf and safarah (Q 98:2; Q 80:13–15) and qalam (Q 68:1; 96:4), strongly suggests that writing was integral to revelation from its inception and that the Prophet personally engaged in writing the scripture (Q 25:5; 29:48). Muhammad is the central referent in both Q 25:5 and Q 29:48, and the temporal qualifier qablihi (“before it”) in Q 29:48 confines the statement to the Prophet’s prior non-exposure to earlier Abrahamic texts. These findings align with philological and codicological research (NEUWIRTH 2019; PUIN 1996; SADEGHI and GOUDARZI 2012; FEDELI 2018) demonstrating the existence of written Qurʾānic material during or immediately after the Prophet’s lifetime. The study also traces how later exegetes—AL-ṬABARĪ, AL-QURṬUBĪ, and IBN KATHĪR—recast ʾummī as “illiterate” through reinterpretations and reinforce the ḥadīth view of prophetic illiteracy as miraculous proof. Engaging with the text directly, the article recenters the Qurʾān’s self-description as al-Kitāb—a text both recited and written—affirming that revelation presupposed literacy, inscription, and textual preservation from the outset.

Keywords: Arabic morphology; early Qurʾānic codification; prophetic literacy; Qurʾānic philology; textual transmission; ummī; written revelation

## Introduction

It is widely believed in mainstream Islam that Prophet Muḥammad was an ummī—an illiterate individual who could neither read nor write. This understanding, however, rests not on Qurʾānic language but on much later exegetical and ḥadīth traditions. Within the Qurʾān itself, the adjective ʾummī (أُمِّيّ) never denotes “unable to read or write.” Linguistically, it describes one who has not been exposed to any previous revelation—a person belonging to a community other than the People of the Book (Jews and Christians).

This article begins by reconstructing that original Qurʾānic meaning through linguistic and textual analysis. It first clarifies how the Qurʾān defines ʾummī and its plural ʾummiyyūn, then turns to internal linguistic evidence—particularly verses Q 25:5; Q 52:2–3; Q 80:13–15; and Q 98:2—which depict the Prophet as directly engaged with written revelation. It subsequently argues, through a pan-textual analysis, that the Qurʾān presents itself as a written text (al-Kitāb) from its very inception—contrary to later extra-Qurʾānic claims that compilation occurred only under the caliphate of ʿUthmān.

Methodologically, this study treats the Qurʾān as a self-referential discourse whose morphology, semantics, and internal cross-references together provide the primary framework for clarifying its key terms and narrative logic—without recourse to external exegetical or historical systems. This linguistic approach does not dismiss the post-Qurʾānic tradition; rather, it adopts a Qurʾān-centric method that first defines its vocabulary (ʾummī, kitāb, ṣuḥuf, qalam) through internal coherence before engaging later commentary. Section 5 elaborates on this framework, situating it within a broader hermeneutical tradition that views linguistic analysis as an act of faithful inquiry rather than polemical revisionism. This pan-textual approach reflects the Qurʾān’s own invitation to examine its internal coherence and consistency (Q 4:82), treating semantic harmony across verses as an interpretive principle rather than subordinating Qurʾānic language to later theological assumptions.

Finally, the paper situates its findings within contemporary philological and codicological scholarship—most notably the works of NEUWIRTH, PUIN, SADEGHI, and FEDELI—which have questioned the conventional chronology of the Qurʾān’s written codification. While fully acknowledging the deep reverence Muslims hold for the Qurʾān and its Messenger, this article seeks to recover—from the Qurʾān itself, as God’s infallible word and “a clarification and detailed explanation of everything” (Q 16:89; cf. Q 6:114–115; Q 7:52; Q 12:111; Q 17:9)—its own linguistic testimony, thereby clarifying how certain theological interpretations evolved over time.

## Q 2:78 — The defining verse

وَمِنْهُمْ أُمِّيُّونَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ الْكِتَابَ إِلَّا أَمَانِيَّ وَإِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَظُنُّونَ

Wa-min-hum ʾummiyyūn lā yaʿlamūna al-kitāba illā ʾamāniyya wa-in hum illā yaẓunnūn.

“And among them are unlettered ones who do not know the Scripture except in wishful thinking, but they are only assuming.” (Q 2:78)

Here the self-referential Qurʾān itself defines ʾummiyyūn as “those who do not know the Book.” Their deficiency lies in ignorance of revelation, not in the inability to read or write. They replace revealed knowledge with ʾamānī (“wishful thinking”) and ẓann (“assumptions”).

The word ʾUmmī (أُمِّيّ) is commonly connected to the root hamza mīm mīm (أ م م)— “source, mother, community”—following the fuʿʿī pattern, which denotes belonging. Thus, ʾummī literally means “one from among the people,” or “one of the common, unscriptured folk.”

## Other Qurʾānic Uses of Ummī and Ummiyyūn

The word ummi occurs in the Qur’an six times, four times as a noun (Q 2:78; Q 3:20, Q 3:75; Q 62:2) and twice as an adjective (Q 7:157-158). Let's look at the remaining five occurrences below:

Verse | Phrase / Context | Description and Semantic Function

Q 3:20 | “…wa-qul lilladhīna ūtū al-kitāba wa-al-ʾummiyyīn…” | Contrasts those given the Book (Ahl al-Kitāb) with al-ʾummiyyīn—the unscriptured peoples lacking prior revelation.

Q 3:75 | “…dhālika bi-annahum qālū laysa ʿalaynā fī al-ʾummiyyīna sabīl…” | Explicit mention of al-ʾummiyyīn (“those without prior scripture”); contrasts Ahl al-Kitāb’s in-group ethics toward the ʾummiyyūn, reinforcing that ʾummiyyūn ≠ illiterate, but “non-scriptured others.”

Q 7:157–158 | “…al-nabiyy al-ʾummī…” “…wa-rasūlihi al-nabiyyi al-ʾummiyy…” | Refers to “the Prophet from among the unscriptured”—one raised without earlier scripture who becomes its first bearer.

Q 62:2 | “Huwa alladhī baʿatha fī al-ʾummiyyīn rasūlan minhum…” | Describes a Messenger raised among the unscriptured nation, tasked with reciting, purifying, and teaching the Book.

Hence the word ʾummiyyīn is contrasted with the people of the book in the Qur’an. Ahl al-Kitāb = people who possess earlier scripture. ʾUmmiyyūn = people who do not.

Thus, when the Qurʾān calls Muhammad al-nabiyy al-ʾummī (Q 7:157), it simply means: “The Prophet from a people without previous revelation.” Before revelation, he shared their ʾummiyyah; afterwards, he became teacher of the Book (Q 62:2).

## No Exposure to Prior Scriptures

Another verse that supports the viewpoint that Muhammad was never exposed to any prior scripture is Q 29:48

وَمَا كُنتَ تَتْلُوا۟ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ مِن كِتَـٰبٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُۥ بِيَمِينِكَ إِذًا لَّٱرْتَابَ ٱلْمُبْطِلُونَ

Wa-mā kunta tatlū min qablihi min kitābin wa-lā takhuṭṭuhu bi-yamīnika idhan la-irtāba al-mubṭilūn.

“And you did not recite before it any scripture, nor did you write it with your right hand, otherwise the falsifiers would have doubted.” (Q 29:48)

Muhammad is being accused of deriving the Qurʾān from earlier Abrahamic texts. The use of qablihi (“before it”) is key, for it temporally binds the argument by indicating not illiteracy but prior non-exposure. The Qurʾān rejects the accusation with this verse, stating that he did not engage with earlier scripture either orally or in written form before this revelation, rather than asserting permanent illiteracy.

## From Ummī to Bearer of the Book

هُوَ الَّذِي بَعَثَ فِي الْأُمِّيِّينَ رَسُولًا مِّنْهُمْ يَتْلُو عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتِهِ وَيُزَكِّيهِمْ وَيُعَلِّمُهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحِكْمَةَ

Huwa alladhī baʿatha fī al-ummiyyīna rasūlan minhum yatlū ʿalayhim āyātihi wa-yuzakkīhim wa-yuʿallimuhumu al-kitāba wa-al-ḥikmata wa-in kānū min qablu la-fī ḍalālin mubīn.

“It is He who has sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves reciting to them His verses and purifying them and teaching them the Book and wisdom - although they were before in clear error.” (Q 62:2)

Logical reasoning would dictate that a messenger who teaches the Book must engage with it textually—since ‘teaching the Book’ (yuʿallimuhum al-kitāb) semantically presupposes familiarity with both its content and medium. The Qurʾān therefore presents Muhammad as transformed from unexposed to revelation into its first reader and teacher.

## Literacy in the Qurʾān: Internal Evidence

The following section examines the Qurʾān itself for evidence that the Prophet Muhammad could both read and write, and that the Qurʾān was being written down from the very beginning of revelation—not merely memorized orally and committed to writing decades later. This internal linguistic evidence challenges the extra-Qurʾānic narrative found in later historical and ḥadīth sources, which claim that the Qurʾān existed only in memory during the Prophet’s lifetime and was written down formally under the caliph ʿUthmān.

In contrast, the Qurʾānic text repeatedly depicts itself as a “kitāb” (written book) right from the very beginning (Q 2:2), described as inscribed, recorded, and transmitted through pages and scribes, indicating that the act of writing was integral to revelation from its earliest moment.

We now turn to the first of these passages—Q 25:5—which linguistically affirms that the Prophet himself was accused of writing the material, not merely reciting it.

## Q 25:5 “He Wrote Them Down”

In Surah al furqān verses 4 and 5, we note the unbelievers are making the accusation of the Qur'an being an invention originating from historical stories as opposed to revelation from God. Clearly the interlocutor being addressed here is Prophet Muhammad. The entrenched belief that Muhammad was ummī—interpreted as “unable to read or write”—has directly influenced how translators render Qurʾān Q 25:5.

وَقَالُوا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ اِكْتَتَبَهَا فَهِيَ تُمْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

Wa-qālū ʾasāṭīru al-awwalīn iktatabahā fa-hiya tumlā ʿalayhi bukratan wa-ʾaṣīlā.

“And they say, "Legends of the former peoples which he has written down, and they are dictated to him morning and afternoon” (Q 25:5)

Here, due to theological orthodoxy, many translators render iktatabahā as “caused to be written,” implying that the Prophet dictated revelation to others. For example, YUSUF ALI (1934) translates Q 25:5 as follows:

“And they say: ‘Tales of the ancients, which he has caused to be written: and they are dictated before him morning and evening.’” (Q 25:5)

The key verb اِكْتَتَبَهَا (iktatabahā) is a Form VIII (اِفْتَعَلَ) verb derived from the root ك ت ب (k-t-b) “to write.”

Form VIII verbs insert a t-infix after the first radical, marking self-directed or reflexive action.

Hence iktatabahā means:

“He wrote them down (for himself).”

If the Qurʾān—whose language is exact to the letter—had intended “he caused them to be written”, it would have used one of the causative patterns instead:

Form II (kat-taba كَتَّبَ) → “to make someone write.” Form IV (ʾaktaba أَكْتَبَ) → “to caused to be written / to dictate.”

Because the verse employs Form VIII, not Form II or IV, the causative reading is grammatically untenable. The morphology itself restricts the meaning to a reflexive act—“he wrote,” not “he had written.”

## Reflexive vs Causative in Arabic Grammar

Before turning to the control example in Q 25:4, it helps to clarify these grammatical categories.

Reflexive → The subject performs the action upon or for itself.

Examples:

ittakhadha (Q 4:125) = “to take for oneself, to adopt.”

iktatabahā (Q 25:5) = “he wrote them down.”

Causative → The subject causes another agent to perform the action.

Examples:

arsala (Q 13:38) = “to cause to go/to send forth.”

aʿlama (Q 2:33) = “to cause someone to know.”

This distinction is foundational in Arabic morphology:

the presence of the prefix ʾa- (Form IV) signals externalization of the act, while the t-infix of Form VIII signals internalization—action reflected back on the subject. Hence, if the meaning was intended to be “he caused to be written down” the Form IV verb would have been aktaba which is not the case in Q 25:5.

Arabic reference grammars consistently note that Form VIII (iftaʿala) verbs typically convey reflexive or middle meanings—actions directed toward or performed for oneself—whereas Form IV (ʾafʿala) verbs are causative, indicating that the subject causes another to perform the act (e.g., ʾaktaba “he caused to write” vs. iktataba “he wrote for himself”; cf. RYDING 2005; OWENS 2013; HOLES 2018).

## Q 25:4 as a Comparative Example

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا إِفْكٌ اِفْتَرَاهُ وَأَعَانَهُ عَلَيْهِ قَوْمٌ آخَرُونَ

Wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū in hādhā illā ʾifkun iftarāhu wa-aʿānahu ʿalayhi qawmun ʾākharūn.

“And those who disbelieve say, "This [Qur'an] is not except a falsehood he invented, and another people assisted him in it." But they have committed an injustice and a lie.” (Q 25:4)

Here, the Qurʾān itself prepares the reader for Q 25:5 by juxtaposing two distinct verb patterns—Form VIII and Form IV—in a single verse. This morphological contrast provides an internal linguistic clue.

Verb (Arabic / Transliteration) | Form & Root | Literal Meaning | Agency Type / Function

اِفْتَرَاهُ (iftarāhu) | Form VIII — ف ر ي (f-r-y) | “He fabricated it himself.” | Reflexive / self-initiated action; the act originates within the subject.

أَعَانَهُ (aʿānahu) | Form IV — ع و ن (ʿ-w-n) | “He caused others to help / they helped him.” | Causative / transitive action; the subject causes another agent to perform the act.

In one verse, the Qurʾān employs both forms side by side to distinguish two kinds of agency:

self-initiated (Form VIII), and

externally caused (Form IV).

This internal contrast showcases the Qurʾān’s morphological precision—when God intends a causative meaning, He employs Form IV (or Form II); when He intends a self-directed action, He employs Form VIII.

This pattern is not incidental but structural. The Qurʾān’s internal verb system distinguishes sharply between Forms IV, II (causative) and VIII (reflexive). That consistency lends decisive grammatical weight to the reflexive reading of (Q 25:5).

This consistent internal morphology confirms that iktatabah—“he wrote them down for himself”—rather than any causative procurement (“he had them written”). The Qurʾān’s own morphology eliminates ambiguity: the Prophet’s opponents accused him of writing, not of employing scribes.

Within the Qurʾān itself, Form VIII (iftaʿala) verbs uniformly express self-directed or reciprocal action; none function causatively. The contrast between iftarāhu (Form VIII, Q 25:4) and aʿānahu (Form IV, same verse) confirms this morphological boundary. Accordingly, iktatabahā (Q 25:5) must mean “he wrote them down for himself,” not “he caused them to be written.” Post-Qurʾānic lexicons expanded the sense to include causative procurement, likely under exegetical pressure to align with the later doctrine of prophetic illiteracy. The Qurʾān’s own linguistic system, however, admits only the reflexive sense.

## Theological Influence on Translation

The widespread rendering of iktatabahā as “he had them written down” in many modern translations does not arise from grammar but from post-Qurʾānic theological presuppositions reinforced by later ḥadīth narratives and exegetical aims.

Among the most frequently cited reports is the account of the first revelation in ṢAḤĪḤ AL-BUKHĀRĪ (4953), where the Angel Jibrīl commands the Prophet, “Read!” (Iqraʾ), and he replies:

“I do not know how to read.” — (ṢAḤĪḤ AL-BUKHĀRĪ, Kitāb Badʾ al-Waḥy, ḥadīth 4953)

This narration has long been interpreted as evidence that the Prophet was unable to read or write. Yet linguistically, the phrase mā ana bi-qāriʾin (“I am not a reader”) does not necessarily mean “I cannot read,” but rather “I have not read [any text or scripture].” The statement therefore reflects the Prophet’s lack of prior engagement with written revelation, not an inherent inability to read.

Another well-known report appears in ṢAḤĪḤ AL-BUKHĀRĪ (1913):

“We are an unlettered nation; we neither write, nor know accounts.” — (ṢAḤĪḤ AL-BUKHĀRĪ, Kitāb al-Ṣawm, ḥadīth 1913)

This hadith describes the cultural condition of the early Arabs (ummiyyūn)—a society without a written tradition of scripture—rather than asserting that the Prophet personally lacked literacy.

Nevertheless, later exegetes extrapolated this collective description to the Prophet himself, interpreting ummī as “illiterate.”

## Exegetical Reframing: Illiteracy as Proof of the Miracle

Early commentators—especially AL-ṬABARĪ (d. 310 AH), followed by AL-QURṬUBĪ (d. 671 AH) and IBN KATHĪR (d. 774 AH)—adopted this understanding of ʾummī to advance a theological argument: that the Qurʾān’s eloquence and literary perfection were miraculous precisely because it was revealed to an “unlettered” man.

In AL-ṬABARĪ’s Tafsīr (on Q 7:157), the Prophet is described as “كان أميًّا لا يكتب” (“he was ummī, he did not write”). AL-QURṬUBĪ follows this, stating that Muḥammad’s illiteracy was part of God’s wisdom, “lest the disbelievers say he learned it from previous scriptures.” IBN KATHĪR reiterates this reading, citing Ibn ʿAbbās: “The ummiyyūn are those who do not write” (on Q 2:78).

This line of reasoning served a theological and apologetic purpose: If the Prophet could not read or write, then the Qurʾān’s literary power must be divine in origin, not human composition.

Thus, the doctrine of prophetic illiteracy became an article of orthodoxy, transforming the word ʾummī from “one without prior scripture” into “one who cannot read or write.”

Furthermore, Q 29:48 became central to this exegetical framework:

وَمَا كُنتَ تَتْلُوا۟ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ مِن كِتَـٰبٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُۥ بِيَمِينِكَ

“And you did not recite before it any scripture, nor did you write it with your right hand…” (Q 29:48)

However, as discussed earlier, the temporal qualifier qablihi (“before it”) confines the statement to the Prophet’s prior non-exposure to earlier Abrahamic texts revealed to Jewish and Christian communities who disputed with him and potentially accused him of deriving the Qurʾān from their scriptures (Q 29:46). IBN KATHĪR, like other exegetes, in his commentary on Q 29:48 narrows the definition of ummī to a prophet “unable to write even one line or one letter” and that “This is how the Messenger of Allah will remain until the Day of Resurrection” without taking into account the time bound nature of the initial clause in Q 29:48.

When read carefully, the verse also implies Muhammad could write with his right hand (bi-yamīnika), a further indication that writing with one’s own hand is a conceivable category for Muhammad; otherwise the clause becomes rhetorically strange.

The verse Q 29:48 coheres naturally with the Qurʾānic usage of ummī and addresses lack of prior scriptural exposure rather than an enduring inability to read or write.

## Impact on Translation of Q 25:5

Once this belief took root, it shaped how Q 25:5 was read and translated. Since exegetes assumed that the Prophet could not write, they reinterpreted iktatabahā—a Form VIII (reflexive) verb meaning “he wrote [it] down for himself”—as if it were Form IV (causative), “he caused it to be written.”

This theological constraint overrode grammar: the morphology of the verb clearly indicates self-action, yet translators rendered it as causative to align with inherited doctrine. As a result, linguistic precision gave way to theological consistency.

A pan-textual linguistic reading of the Qurʾān, however, restores the original sense: the Prophet’s opponents accused him of personally writing down revelation that was being dictated (tumlā) to him, which they dismissed as “tales of the ancients.” The term tumlā reinforces the image of a message both heard and inscribed, directly by its recipient.

In summary, the belief that Muhammad could not read or write originates not from the Qurʾān’s language but from post-Qurʾānic exegesis and ḥadīth interpretation. Exegetes sought to elevate the Prophet’s illiteracy into a miraculous proof of divine revelation, unintentionally distorting how verses like Q 25:5 were read and translated. Grammar, however, preserves the original meaning: iktatabahā— “he wrote them down for himself.”

Having established from morphology that the Prophet personally engaged in writing, we now examine the Qurʾān’s broader self-presentation as a written revelation. Further textual evidence within the Qurʾān is provided showing that the revelation was being written down from the very beginning, not compiled posthumously after the Prophet’s lifetime.

## 3.1. Q 98:2 — “Reciting Pure Pages”

رَسُولٌۭ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ يَتْلُوا۟ صُحُفًۭا مُّطَهَّرَةًۭ

Rasūlun mina llāhi yatlū ṣuḥufan muṭahharah.

“A messenger from God, reciting purified scriptures.” (Q 98:2)

The triliteral root ṣ-ḥ-f (ص-ح-ف) connotes to spread open, inscribe, display in writing. Ṣuḥuf (plural of ṣaḥīfah) literally means “pages” or “scrolls.”

When the plural ṣuḥuf appears, its Qurʾānic usage overwhelmingly refers to inscribed, textual forms of revelation — not oral, metaphorical, or eschatological concepts — with only rare exceptions. The following table shows that except for Q 81:10 (eschatological “records of deeds”) and Q 43:71 (platters of gold), every occurrence of ṣuḥuf in the Qurʾān unambiguously denotes written, textual material.

Verse | Phrase | Immediate Context | Semantic Function

Q 20:133 | “Aw lam ta’tihim bayyinat mā fī ṣuḥuf al-ūlā?” — “Has there not come to them clear evidence of what was in the earlier records?” | The verse challenges deniers who demand new miracles despite already having al-ṣuḥuf al-ʾūlā (earliest scriptures). | Clearly textual — refers to previous written revelations.

Q 53:36–37 | “Am lam yunaḇḇaʾ bimā fī ṣuḥuf Mūsā wa-Ibrāhīm…” | Invokes the written teachings of Moses and Abraham. | Written scriptures—explicitly described as ṣuḥuf belonging to named prophets.

Q 74:52 | “Kullu mriʾin minhum yurīdu an yuʾtā ṣuḥufan munashsharah.” | Describes arrogant deniers who demand that scrolls be physically handed to each of them. | Literal writing—they want tangible scrolls opened/unrolled for themselves.

Q 80:13–15 | “Fī ṣuḥufin mukarramah marfūʿah muṭahharah bi-aydī safarah…” | Portrays revelation as inscribed in honorable, purified pages handled by scribes. | Explicitly written pages, handled and transmitted through scribal mediation.

Q 87:18–19 | “Ṣuḥuf Ibrāhīm wa Mūsā.” | Cites the enduring written legacy of previous prophets. | Again, written revelation, archetypal “pages.”

Accordingly, Q 98:2 — “reciting purified ṣuḥuf” — implies engagement with actual written pages. This meaning is linguistically reinforced by every other revelatory use of ṣuḥuf across the Qurʾān. The Qurʾānic semantic field of ṣuḥuf thus confirms that writing was intrinsic to revelation from the outset.

## 3.2. Q 52:2–3 — “A Book inscribed on Parchment”

وَالطُّورِ وَكِتَابٍ مَسْطُورٍ فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ

Wa-al-ṭūr(i) wa-kitābin masṭūr(in) fī raqqin manshūr(in)

“By the Mount, and by a Book inscribed, In parchment spread open.” (Q 52:2–3)

Here, revelation is explicitly tied to writing — a kitāb masṭūr, a book inscribed upon raqq, meaning parchment or fine writing skin. The adjective masṭūr (مَسْطُور) derives from the root س-ط-ر (s-ṭ-r), meaning “to write in lines or to arrange in order by writing.”

As recorded in Lane’s Arabic–English Lexicon (Vol. 4, p. 1319):

“سَطَرَ — He wrote, or drew lines, or arranged in writing; hence masṭūr signifies ‘written, lined, inscribed.’ The term applies to what is set forth in writing, arranged in lines upon a page.”

Similarly, raqq (رَقّ) denotes a thin surface prepared for writing — usually fine parchment made from animal skin (Lane Vol. 1, p. 1164).

This oath (Q 52:2–3) places the Book within a material and scribal context: revelation is already inscribed, existing in written form, not merely as a recited utterance. The Qurʾān therefore presents itself as a textual revelation, implying a Prophet familiar with the concept and medium of writing — a message both preserved and manifested through the act of inscription.

## 3.3. Q 80:13–16 — “In honored pages, in the hands of scribes”

فِي صُحُفٍ مُّكَرَّمَةٍ مَّرْفُوعَةٍ مُّطَهَّرَةٍ بِأَيْدِي سَفَرَةٍ كِرَامٍ بَرَرَةٍ

Fī ṣuḥufin mukarramah, marfūʿatin muṭahharah, bi-aydī safarah kirāmin bararah.

“upon pages high-honoured, uplifted, purified, by the hands of scribes — noble, pious” (Q 80:13–16)

The revelation is portrayed as ṣuḥuf — “written pages” or “scrolls” — handled by safarah, meaning scribes. The term safarah derives from the triliteral root س-ف-ر (s-f-r), which in classical Arabic denotes “to uncover, to make manifest, to bring to light.”

As LANE’s Lexicon notes (Vol. 1, p. 1413):

“سَفَرَ — He uncovered, revealed, or made manifest a thing that was concealed; hence al-sifr (book) is so called because it discloses or makes manifest knowledge written therein.”

This linguistic background shows that sāfir —and its plural safarah — describes those who make revelation manifest, i.e., scribes who inscribe divine communication in written form. Hence the verse reinforces revelation as a written and textual phenomenon, not merely an oral recital. It depicts a process of transmission involving scribes of high moral standing, suggesting that the Qurʾān was being written down, copied, and preserved from its earliest phase of revelation, ready for dissemination among the believers.

## 3.4. Q 68:1 and Q 96:4 — “By the Pen”

The vivid imagery described in Q 68:1 and Q 96:4 indirectly provides further evidence of a writing culture within the community that lacked exposure to any prior revelation.

نٓ وَٱلْقَلَمِ وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ

Nūn. Wa-al-qalami wa-mā yasṭurūn.

“Nūn. By the pen and what they inscribe.” (68:1)

ٱلَّذِى عَلَّمَ بِٱلْقَلَمِ

Alladhī ʿallama bi-al-qalam.

“Who taught by the pen.” (96:4)

These verses suggest literacy within the recipients of the Qurʾān and that writing was intrinsic to revelation from the very beginning.

## 3.5. Q 2:282 — “Write it down”

This is the longest verse in the Qurʾān and it commands that contracts be written down. It exhorts believers to “let a scribe write,” “do not refuse to write” and “do not be weary of writing it, whether big or small” suggesting that there was a culture of reading and writing during the time of Muhammad. There were scribes who wrote and preservation through writing was common in serious matters.

This suggests that a civilization capable of documenting complex debt contracts would obviously document revelation.

## 3.6. The Qurʾān as “al-Kitāb”: A Written Revelation

Beyond individual verses, the Qurʾān repeatedly refers to itself as al-Kitāb — “the Book.” This term alone signals a written and recorded reality, not a purely oral composition.

The root ك-ت-ب (k-t-b) means “to write, to inscribe, to record.” As LANE’s Lexicon explains (Vol. 7, p. 2620):

“كَتَبَ — He wrote, or inscribed characters or words; he caused to be recorded in writing.”

Thus, when the Qurʾān opens with ذَٰلِكَ الْكِتَابُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ (dhālika al-kitāb lā rayba fīh, Q 2:2) — “That is the Book in which there is no doubt” — it identifies itself explicitly as a written text. The demonstrative dhālika points to something present and recognizable, while kitāb denotes a documented record. The numerous occurrences of al-kitāb throughout the Qurʾān (e.g., Q 2:2; Q 3:3; Q 6:7; Q 17:58; Q 29:48) reinforce this self-understanding: revelation was inscribed, preserved, and transmitted in written form from the outset.

This stands in contrast to later extra-Qurʾānic traditions that depict the Qurʾān as a primarily oral corpus compiled only under the caliph ʿUthmān. The Qurʾān’s own language leaves no room for that assumption — it presents itself as a text already written, read, and engaged with by the Prophet and his community.

## 4. Previous Scholarship and Context

Modern Qurʾānic philology has increasingly challenged the traditional chronology that confines the Qurʾān’s written codification to the caliphate of ʿUthmān. As ANGELIKA NEUWIRTH notes,

“Evidence of old Qurʾān codices (see PUIN 1996) as well as new philological (DE BLOIS 2002) and historical (AMMAN; DONNER 1998; DOSTAL 1991; KRONE 1992) studies have provided strong arguments in favor of the Qurʾān’s emergence from an Arabian environment and of an early date of the Qurʾān redaction, thus advocating for the fixation of the text in the shape transmitted to us.” – In the Blackwell Companion to the Qurʾān, ed. ANDREW RIPPIN (Chichester: Wiley-Blackwell, 2009), 141.

She situates the Qurʾān within the literary and scriptural culture of Late Antiquity, arguing that its textual form “emerged from and dialogued with the written cultures of Jewish and Christian Late Antiquity” (NEUWIRTH 2019, 3).

Codicological research by PUIN and others reinforces this reassessment of the Qurʾān’s early written transmission. As NEUWIRTH (2009) observes, GERD R. PUIN’s study of the Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsests revealed textual features characteristic of first-century AH writing:

“A few of these Qurʾānic manuscripts displayed signs of great antiquity, allowing them to be placed with a degree of certitude into the first century of the Hijra” (PUIN 1996, 110).

He concludes that “the textual history of the Qurʾān is more complex than traditionally assumed,” implying contemporaneous written activity during the Prophet’s lifetime (ibid., 112).

Palimpsest studies of Ṣanʿāʾ 1 have further shown that the parchment dates from the first half of the seventh century A.D. and that its text does not belong to the ʿUthmānic textual tradition. The lower writing represents “the only known manuscript of a non-ʿUthmānic text type,” identified through palaeographic and art-historical analysis. Radiocarbon testing assigns the parchment—and hence the lower codex—to a period before A.D. 671 with a probability of 99 percent (before 661 with 95.5 percent; before 646 with 75 percent) (SADEGHI and GOUDARZI 2012, 1–3). This places it significantly earlier than other Qurʾānic manuscripts dated so far.

The Birmingham manuscript (University of Birmingham, Mingana 1572a) has likewise been radiocarbon-dated to 568–645 CE CE (95.4% probability), overlapping with the Prophet’s lifetime. While the dating applies to the writing material rather than the ink, the manuscript nonetheless constitutes one of the earliest extant witnesses to the Qurʾānic text (FEDELI 2018).

Such evidence points to an active scribal milieu during or immediately after the Prophet’s life rather than an exclusively oral preservation awaiting later codification. Historically and empirically, the claim of early written transmission can now be tested and verified: the Qurʾān’s text has remained consistent from the earliest surviving manuscripts of the seventh century CE to modern printed editions.

Collectively, these studies lend empirical weight to a linguistic reading of the Qurʾān that recognizes writing as intrinsic to revelation. The convergence of internal linguistic evidence and external manuscript data supports the thesis advanced here: that the Qurʾān was being written from its inception, and that later notions of prophetic illiteracy or delayed compilation arose from theological rather than linguistic premises.

## 5. Methodological Reflection and Intent

This study does not seek to diminish the long-standing reverence Muslims hold for the Qurʾān and its Messenger; rather, it seeks to affirm and elevate that reverence by returning to the Qurʾān’s own linguistic evidence. By allowing the text to speak in its own terms, the study clarifies how certain theological interpretations—such as prophetic illiteracy and delayed codification—evolved historically and came to replace the Qurʾān’s own self-description over time.

For more than a millennium, Muslim exegetes, philologists, and historians have sought to explain the miraculous nature of the Qurʾān. In doing so, they developed interpretive frameworks that emphasized divine intervention over human agency—among them the notion that the Prophet was “illiterate” and that the Qurʾān’s preservation occurred only through a later official codification. These explanations were not born out of malice but from a deep desire to defend revelation against skepticism and to highlight its divine origin.

However, linguistic and textual analysis of the Qurʾān itself reveals a more nuanced picture: one that strengthens, rather than weakens, the miracle of revelation. If the Qurʾān shows that the Prophet engaged directly with writing, this does not diminish the divine message—it instead attests to a revelation perfectly suited to its Messenger and his context. Similarly, evidence that the Qurʾān was written during the Prophet’s lifetime affirms the very precision and care with which divine words were recorded and transmitted.

Re-examining inherited assumptions, therefore, is not an act of defiance but an act of faithfulness—to the Qurʾān’s own language and testimony, which affirms that the gathering, recitation and clarification of the message originate from the divine (Q 75:16-19).

“Upon Us is its gathering and its recitation [...] Then upon Us is its clarification.” (75:16-19)

The intention here is not to replace one dogma with another, but to invite readers to engage the text as it speaks, without intermediaries. Such inquiry belongs to a long and respected tradition within Islam, where scholars—from al-Zamakhsharī to Muʿtazilite grammarians—pursued meaning through language, reason, and evidence.

A comparable pan-textual linguistic approach has been applied in a separate study of Qurʾān Q 65:4, demonstrating that the verse—often cited in debates on child marriage—when read contextually, upholds marriage as a contractual union between mature, accountable partners rather than as a sanction of prepubescent marriage (SAMIN and MALIK 2025). Both studies exemplify how a Qurʾān-centric linguistic method can clarify the text’s ethical and theological coherence without reliance on later exegetical constructions grounded in assumed conclusions, revealing that the Qurʾān’s internal linguistic architecture is ethically and intellectually self-consistent once freed from inherited interpretation.

Ultimately, acknowledging that some post-Qurʾānic interpretations were shaped by historical apologetics does not diminish but rather elevates the sanctity of revelation. It reminds us that human interpretation, however sincere, remains fallible when measured against the Qurʾān’s own morphological and syntactic precision. As the Qurʾān declares:

“Falsehood cannot approach it from before it or from behind it; [it is] a revelation from a [Lord who is] Wise and Praiseworthy.” (Q 41:42)

Conclusion — Qurʾānic Language vs. Later Tradition

A pan-textual linguistic reading of the Qurʾān stands in tension with later exegetical and ḥadīth-based portrayals of an “illiterate Prophet.” Within the Qurʾān, the word ʾummī means “unscriptured,” not illiterate (Q 2:78). Muḥammad was ummī only in the sense of being raised among a people “who did not know the Book” (Q 62:2).

Once revelation began, he became the reader, teacher, and transmitter of a written Book. Verses such as (Q 25:5; 98:2; 52:2–3; 80:13–15; 29:48; 2:282; 68:1; 96:4) depict a Prophet operating within a culture of writing and reading.

The Qurʾān presents itself as a written text (al-kitāb) from the very beginning which is in tension with extra-Qurʾānic historical claims that it was compiled only later during the reign of ʿUthmān. This internal Qurʾānic evidence is consistent with and not contradicted by the Ṣanʿāʾ 1 and Birmingham Manuscript radiocarbon dating record discussed earlier.

The Qurʾān’s own morphology, syntax, and semantics therefore indicate that Muḥammad could read and write, and that his “unlettered” status referred not to literacy but to the absence of prior exposure to any scripture. Its internal evidence—from the precision of its verbs (iktatabahā), to its repeated self-reference as kitāb, to its vivid imagery of ṣuḥuf (pages), safarah (scribes) writing with their hands and qalam (pen)—reveals a revelation conceived, transmitted, and preserved through writing.

The Qurʾān self-describes revelation through terms that intrinsically presuppose writing—kitāb, ṣuḥuf, raqq, masṭūr, safarah, qalam. In its own language, the Qurʾān affirms a Prophet who could read and write—not an “unlettered” man reciting words he could not comprehend, but a dutiful Messenger entrusted with reading, writing, teaching, and preserving “the Book in which there is no doubt.”
