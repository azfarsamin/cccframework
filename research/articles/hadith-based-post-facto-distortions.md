# Hadith-Based Post-facto Distortions

## Introduction

There is a general understanding in mainstream Islam that hadith literature is employed to “explain” the Qur’an and that it provides a practicing Muslim with details that he believes are missing from God’s revelation received by Muhammad. The reality is quite different. As established in Chapter 1, any truth-claim must pass the tests of Coherence, Correspondence, and Calibration. And as I argued earlier, the Qur’ān possesses an internal linguistic and structural architecture that resists external distortion.

This chapter applies those same principles to the post-Qur’ānic hadith corpus. I will show that hadith literature not only fails the CCC tests but also introduces contradictions that violate the Qur’ān’s preserved semantic and structural system. In example after example, these post-facto texts cannot integrate into the Qur’ān’s coherence network, and the Qur’ān’s built-in calibration mechanisms immediately expose the misalignment.

The implications of this additional theology—superimposed centuries later onto the Qur’an—are profound: it transforms God’s message from one centered on serving God alone into a system that venerates Muhammad as someone who could not err, who could foresee the future, who could declare things lawful and unlawful, and who will, on the Day of Judgment, intercede on behalf of Muslims. The secondary literature also transforms him from a messenger whose primary duty was to warn his community, to someone who allegedly went against what was revealed to him and made claims never authorized by God.

In this light, the hadith corpus becomes a stress test for the CCC model. Whenever later literature introduces concepts that are alien to the Qur’ān’s worldview, the Qur’ān’s coherence (linguistic precision), correspondence (moral and empirical reality), and calibration (self-correcting cross-referential structure) instantly reject the intrusion. This is precisely the behavior predicted earlier: a divinely preserved text that is tamper-resistant by design.

The next sections illustrate the tension between the Qur’ān and the literature compiled 250–300 years later and attributed to Muhammad—texts he never saw, edited, or authorized.

## No Compulsion in Religion — Apostasy Punishment Contradicts the Qur’ān

A widely accepted belief in hadith-based Islam is that anyone who “leaves the religion” must be put to death. This claim is rooted not in the Qur’ān, but in a handful of hadith reports that declare that “Whoever changes his religion, kill him.”

Sahih al-Bukhari 3017 (Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar / Book of Jihaad)

Narrated by Ibn 'Abbas: The Prophet said, "Whoever changes his religion, kill him."

Sunan an-Nasa'i 4059, 4061, 4062, 4063, 4064 (Kitab Tahrim al-Dam / The Book of The Prohibition of Blood)

Narrated by Ibn 'Abbas: "The Messenger of Allah [ﷺ] said: 'Whoever changes his religion, execute him.'"

Sunan Ibn Majah 2535 (Kitab al-Hudud / Book of Legal Punishments)

Narrated by Ibn 'Abbas: "The Messenger of Allah [ﷺ] said: 'Whoever changes his religion, kill him.'"

Sunan Abi Dawud 4351 (Kitab al-Hudud)

Recorded under the chapter regarding the ruling on one who turns back from Islam (Apostasy).

Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1458 (Kitab al-Hudud)

Recorded with similar wording via Ibn 'Abbas.

Musnad Ahmad ibn Hanbal 2558

Recorded across several chains passing through Ibn 'Abbas.

This teaching has shaped mainstream law schools and continues to influence legal rulings in many Muslim-majority societies. Apostasy is treated as a capital crime, and people are threatened, punished, or killed for changing their beliefs. While modern apologists often attempt to reframe these Hadiths as laws against political treason, the text of the reports contains no such political qualifiers.

The Qur’ān teaches the exact opposite. It establishes absolute freedom of belief, the right to doubt, the right to reject, and the right to change one’s position — without any worldly penalty. Judgment belongs to God alone.

“There is no compulsion in religion.” (Q 2:256)

One of the clearest and most universal declarations in the Qur’ān. No one can force a person to enter faith, remain in faith, or return to faith.

People are free to believe or disbelieve:

“Let whoever wills believe, and let whoever wills disbelieve.” (Q 18:29)

A direct affirmation of freedom of conscience.

Even the Prophet cannot compel belief:

“Would you compel people until they become believers?”
 “…No soul can believe except by God’s permission.” (Q 10:99–100)

God Himself tells Muhammad he cannot compel faith — not with force, not with pressure, not with penalties.

Muhammad is not a controller over people:

“So remind — you are only a reminder. You are not a controller over them.” (Q 88:21–22)

If Muhammad cannot control people’s beliefs, no one else can. His responsibility was limited to warning those around him.

The Qur’ān describes apostasy many times, but never once prescribes a worldly punishment:

“Those who believed, then disbelieved, then believed, then disbelieved, then increased in disbelief…” (Q 4:137)

According to the Qur’ān, a person can move in and out of belief multiple times — and still be left alone.
No penalty.
No arrest.
No execution.

Why?
Because:

“…God will not forgive them, nor guide them…” (Q 4:137)

The judgment is with God, not human courts. People will differ until the end of time:

“Had your Lord willed, He could have made all humanity one community — but they will continue to differ…” (Q 11:118)

Difference is part of divine design, not a punishable offense.

According to the Qur’ān:

Faith cannot be forced.

Apostasy is not a reason to prosecute heretics.

People can believe, disbelieve, change belief, or reject belief.

Only God judges religious choices, not man-made religions that require conformance.

Even the Prophet had no authority to compel belief.

Religious coercion contradicts divine intent.

According to hadith-based theology:

Apostasy is a capital crime.

People must remain in the religion defined by the majority.

Muhammad supposedly ordered their execution.

Human courts take over God’s role of judgment.

These positions are incompatible. More often than not such legal rulings are created to punish those who are critical of those who use religion as a tool to consolidate power. The criteria of disbelief is decided by social consensus and institutional authority.

This forms a major contradiction between hadith-based Islam and the Qur’ān’s core principle of freedom of belief.

## The Qur’ān Rejects Stoning (Rajm) and Prescribes Lashes for Adultery (Zina)

Hadith-based Islam teaches that those who commit adultery must be stoned to death (rajm). This ruling appears in multiple hadith collections and is treated by classical jurists as a central part of Islamic law.

But the Qur’ān never commands stoning.

In fact, the Qur’ān explicitly provides the punishment for zina — for both adultery and fornication — and it is not death.

The Qur’ān also contains internal evidence that stoning cannot be part of divine law.

The Qur’ān’s punishment for zina is lashes — not death:

“The unchaste woman and the unchaste man — flog each of them with a hundred lashes…” (Q 24:2)

This is the only Qur’ānic punishment for zina. There is no additional category, no separate punishment for married vs unmarried, no hidden verse, and no exception. Q 24:2 uses the general nouns al-zāniyatu wa-l-zānī ("the unchaste female and the unchaste male") without any qualification for marital status. If God wanted stoning, God would have said so.

It is nothing short of ironic that rajm (stoning) appears only as a punishment threatened by disbelievers. Every place where stoning appears in the Qur’ān is in the mouths of opponents of the prophets — never as God’s command:

Q 18:20 — a threat to the youths of the cave

Q 19:46 — Abraham threatened by his father

Q 26:116 — Noah’s opponents threaten stoning

Q 36:18 — messengers threatened by their people

Q 44:20 — Moses threatened with stoning

In each instance:

Stoning is a pagan punishment used against prophets. It is never commanded by God.

The strongest internal refutation of stoning — Q 4:25. When discussing believing maid women who are married and commit sexual misconduct, the Qur’ān says:

“…upon them is half the punishment of free women.” (Q 4:25)

The punishment for zina is 100 lashes (Q 24:2). Half of 100 is 50. It is clear the lashes can be halved, not stoning to death. This single verse mathematically and legally eliminates rajm as a valid punishment. If stoning were from God, Q 4:25 would be impossible.

According to the Qur’ān:

The punishment for zina is 100 lashes.

It applies equally to men and women.

Maidservants receive half that amount.

Stoning is never God’s law.

Stoning is always shown as a threat used by disbelievers against prophets.

According to hadith-based theology:

Married adulterers are to be stoned to death.

Stoning is treated as a prophetic legal ruling.

External reports override the explicit Qur’ānic punishment.

These two positions cannot be reconciled.

The Qur’an’s Coherence axis rejects any ruling that violates its internal legal logic—such as the impossibility of ‘half of stoning’—and its preservation mechanism (Q 4:82, 15:9) blocks the import of alien punishments like rajm, exposing them as external intrusions rather than divine law.

## The Qur’an is ‘Incomplete’ Framing

The hadith literature advanced the "Goat Ate the Quranic Verses" narrative to smuggle in the verses on stoning (rajm) and adult suckling (reḍāʿ al-kabīr) and suggests that they were written on a sheet under ʿĀʾishah's bed, but were eaten by a domestic goat while the Prophet was being prepared for burial, causing them to be lost from the text.

Sunan Ibn Mājah (Hadith 1944 — Kitāb al-Nikāḥ / The Book of Marriage):

Narrated by ʿĀʾishah: "The Verse of stoning and of suckling an adult ten times was revealed, and the paper was with me under my pillow. When the Messenger of Allah [ﷺ] died and we were preoccupied with his death, a tame goat came in and ate it."

In Sahih Muslim 1452, it is mentioned that it was initially ten adult sucklings, which was later abrogated to five known sucklings which adds another layer of incoherence.

The claim is that the divine scripture—guaranteed protection by God Himself — was subject to accidental deletion by a domestic farm animal during the Prophet's funeral.

Classical jurists invoke the doctrine of abrogation to assert that God intended for these verses to be removed from the physical manuscript while keeping the laws binding. To accept the logical absurdity of this ruling and the Hadith narrative requires accepting the following:

God revealed a law in writing.

God decided the text itself should no longer be part of His scripture.

God still expects human judges to apply the death penalty based on an unwritten, invisible ghost-verse that He removed from the book.

It is the theological equivalent of telling a judge,

"I have a contract that mandates you pay me a million dollars. I don't have the paper because my dog ate it, and God wanted my dog to eat it, but you still have to pay me."

No court on Earth would accept that logic, yet traditionalist jurisprudence built an entire capital punishment framework around it.

This fabricated story, alien to the primary text, is contradicted by many verses (Q 15:9; 41:42; 6:115; 12:111; 4:25).

Q 15:9 guarantees:

"Indeed, it is We who sent down the Dhikr, and indeed, We will be its guardian."

Q 41:42 affirms:

"Falsehood cannot approach it—neither from before it nor from behind it."

Q 6:115 states:

“And perfected is the word of thy Lord in truth and justice; there is none to change His words.”

The traditionalists create a fatal epistemological trap for themselves:

EITHER the text was abrogated by God: Why enforce a law that God deliberately removed from His final, complete guidance?

OR the text was lost by accident (e.g., the goat narrative): Then God failed to preserve His Book, violating the verses above.

## Hadith Extrapolations about Muhammad

### Muhammad was Sinless/Inerrant

Many Muslims grow up being taught that Muhammad was flawless, sinless, or incapable of making mistakes. This belief—shaped largely by hadith literature and later theology—became central in both Sunni and Shia thought, where the Prophet is often described as ma‘soom, meaning divinely protected from error.

But the Qur’ān itself gives us a very different picture.

The Qur’ān presents Muhammad as a human being who was guided by God, protected by God, and honored by God—but not as a faultless or error-proof figure. In fact, the Qur’ān openly records several moments where God gently but firmly corrects him.

Below are key examples.

“That God may forgive you your past and future shortcomings…” (Q 48:2)

You do not forgive someone who is incapable of error. The verse directly acknowledges that he had faults which God pardoned.

“…seek forgiveness for your fault…” (Q 47:19)

The command is clear. A sinless man does not need forgiveness.

At a time of deep grief over people rejecting the message, God tells him:

“…so do not be among the ignorant.” (Q 6:35)

This is a strong admonition. It is guidance, correction, and a warning wrapped together.

In these next verses, God clearly admonishes the Prophet and his companions for their desire to take captives and gain worldly benefit:

“It is not for a prophet to have captives until he has battled strenuously in the land. You desire the goods of this world, and God desires the Hereafter… Had it not been for a decree from God that had already preceded, a great punishment would have touched you for what you took.” (Q 8:67–68)

Were it not for God’s decree—severe consequences would have followed. This is clearly corrective language, not the language of perfection.

In the next verse, Muhammad is chided for not using the proper criteria to give leave to those who did not want to join in the battle:

“May God pardon you! Why did you give them permission…” (Q 9:43)

This is a direct admonition: he acted prematurely.

In Q 9:113, Muhammad is told after it became clear that asking for forgiveness for those who persisted in associating partners with God was not appropriate.

“It is not for the Prophet and those who believe to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they are close relatives, after it has become clear to them that they are companions of the Fire.” (Q 9:113)

He is warned repeatedly against compromising or yielding in Q 17:73–75. These verses warn that if he had even slightly inclined toward the opponents, he would have faced severe consequences.

In another episode involving his wives he is again admonished by God:

“O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful for you…” (Q 66:1)

Here he is directly questioned by God for imposing unnecessary restrictions seeking to please his wives.

He is corrected for frowning at a blind man — Q 80:1–2. The Qur’ān openly criticizes him for showing displeasure toward a sincere seeker while turning toward wealthy opponents. No attempt is made to hide this. This transparency itself is part of the Qur’ān’s moral power.

The Qur’an is clear: the recipient of the Qur’an was astray and then guided.

“He found you astray and guided you.” (Q 93:7)

These verses matter because a text that hides the mistakes of its messenger is elevating him to a status that is reserved for God alone. The Qur’ān does the opposite: it puts those corrections on full display, showing that Muhammad grew, learned, adjusted, and submitted to God just like every other believer.

The Qur’ān never portrays him as an untouchable, flawless icon. It portrays him as a devoted servant of God—human enough to err, humble enough to be corrected, and sincere enough to accept that correction. This picture stands in direct contrast to later theological claims that the Prophet was sinless or incapable of error.

The Qur’ān honors Muhammad not by denying his humanity, but by showing how he grew through divine guidance. This makes his example more meaningful—not less.

### Muhammad Was Not Given a Higher Status Over Other Messengers

A common belief in mainstream Islam—shaped by hadith literature and later theology—is that Muhammad holds a rank above all other messengers. But the Qur’ān repeatedly rejects the notion that any messenger should be elevated above the rest. Across several verses, God commands believers to treat all messengers with equal respect and equal status:

“We make no distinction between any of them.” (Q 2:136)

“We make no distinction between any of His messengers.” (Q 2:285)

“Say: We believe in God… and in what was given to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes… We make no distinction between any of them.” (Q 3:84)

“Those who believe in God and His messengers and do not make a distinction between any of them—God will give them their rewards.” (Q 4:152)

The Qur’ān’s teaching is unambiguous:

All messengers stand on the same footing. No human being—Muhammad included—is allowed to be elevated above the rest. That he was the seal of the prophets does not elevate him above the others; it simply marks him as the final messenger in a sequence that remains equal in status.

This directly contradicts the later practice of:

invoking Muhammad’s name as a requirement for entering Islam, nowhere found in the Qur’an (Q 3:18; 3:19; 2:136; 42:13),

adding his name to the shahada to testify that he is the messenger, an utterance ascribed to hypocrites (Q 63:1).

giving him privileges not granted in revelation,

treating him as spiritually superior by default.

While it is true that there are messengers who were given preference and a higher degree over others including Muhammad (Q 2:253), that does not authorize later followers to elevate him into a unique devotional or quasi-divine position invoking his name alongside God as part of requirement of faith. These actions, predicated on hadith literature, clearly violate Q 39:45 and Q 72:18.

“And when God alone is remembered, the hearts of those who believe not in the Hereafter shrink with aversion; but when those besides Him are remembered, then they rejoice. (Q 39:45)

And the places of worship belong to God — so call not with God to anyone. (Q 72:18)

The Qur’ān’s message is consistent: the honor of messengership itself is the distinction—not the personality of the messenger. The Qur’ān asks believers to follow the message, not to rank the messengers.

The next issue is closely related: the claim that Muhammad possessed knowledge of the unseen or could predict future events—a notion the Qur’ān explicitly rejects.

### Muhammad Did Not Know the Unseen or the Future

Another widespread belief shaped by hadith literature is that Muhammad knew the unseen, foretold specific future events, and even predicted details of the end of time. Entire branches of Islamic eschatology are built on the assumption that the Prophet possessed knowledge of things hidden from ordinary human beings.

But the Qur’ān firmly rejects this idea.

Muhammad is repeatedly instructed to declare that he does not possess the knowledge of the unseen (al-ghayb), nor does he know what will happen tomorrow, nor does he have any control over what becomes of him or of others.

Hadith literature contains stories such as the ‘ashra mubashshara’—ten companions supposedly promised Paradise—which rest entirely on extra-Qurʾānic reports.

The Qur’ān firmly rejects the idea that Muhammad had knowledge of anyone’s ultimate fate — including his own.

“Say: I do not say to you that I possess God’s treasures, nor do I know the unseen…” (Q 6:50)

“They ask you about the Hour… Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord… Say: I do not possess for myself any benefit or harm, except what God wills. If I had knowledge of the unseen, I would have increased in good, and no harm would have touched me.” (Q 7:187–188)

“I do not tell you that I possess God’s treasures, nor do I know the unseen…” (Q 11:31)

“It is not the speech of a soothsayer. Little do you remember.” (Q 69:42)

These verses establish a consistent, unbroken truth:

Muhammad did not know the unseen, did not predict future events, and had no knowledge of the timing of the Hour (Q 79:42-45).

This completely contradicts the hadith-based belief that:

he had detailed knowledge of the end times,

he predicted specific historical events,

he possessed supernatural sight into the unseen.

According to the Qur’ān, only God knows the unseen (Q 6:59; 72:26). Muhammad’s role was to deliver the message, not to act as a fortune-teller or an eschatological narrator.

The next issue follows naturally: the belief that Muhammad had authority to add to or modify revelation—an idea the Qur’ān rejects decisively.

### Muhammad Was Not Allowed to Add, Alter, or Invent Teachings

A central claim in hadith-based Islam is that Muhammad had the authority to declare new rulings, make things lawful or unlawful, and speak in God’s name outside the Qur’ān. This belief underlies the entire framework of “Sunnah legislation,” where prophetic sayings are treated as an independent source of law.

But the Qur’ān explicitly denies that Muhammad had any such authority. The messenger’s role was strictly defined: to convey only what was revealed, to follow only revelation, and to never speak in God’s name from himself. The Qur’ān is absolutely clear on this point. He could not fabricate sayings in God’s name:

“If he had dared to invent any sayings in Our name, We would have seized him by the right hand, then severed his aorta.” (Q 69:44–47)

This is one of the strongest warnings in the entire Qur’ān. It leaves no room for the idea that Muhammad could produce independent religious teachings.

He could not issue his own religious rulings:

“Say: Have you considered what God has sent down for you as provision, and you made some of it lawful and some unlawful? Say: Did God give you permission, or are you inventing lies against God?” (Q 10:59)

If no one is allowed to issue independent religious rulings without God’s permission, Muhammad certainly was not allowed to either.

Muhammad could not:

invent religious sayings,

issue new laws,

make things lawful or unlawful,

legislate independently of revelation,

or add anything to the religion.

He was expressly rebuked for making things impermissible to please his wives (Q 66:1). This directly contradicts the hadith-based idea that Muhammad acted as a secondary lawgiver. The Qur’ān allows no space for an additional layer of revelation or independent prophetic legislation.

Muhammad interceding on behalf of Muslims on the day of judgment is the next topic of tension between tradition and revelation.

### Muhammad Cannot Intercede on Judgment Day

A common belief in hadith-based Islam is that Muslims who enter Hell will eventually be forgiven and spared from punishment because Muhammad will intercede on their behalf. Entire theologies are built around the idea that a person’s recitation of the shahada guarantees eventual salvation, regardless of their deeds.

But the Qur’ān repeatedly rejects this idea.

The Qur’ān states openly that:

no soul can help another,

no intercession is accepted without God’s permission,

the guilty cannot escape punishment,

and those condemned to Hell will remain there eternally unless God Himself wills otherwise — not due to someone else’s intervention.

The Qur’ān dismantles the belief in guaranteed prophetic rescue.

False assurances of salvation are condemned:

“And they say: ‘The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.’
 Say: Have you taken a promise from God? Then God will not break His promise. Or do you say about God what you do not know?” (Q 2:80)

This verse directly condemns the belief that one can sin yet be assured of limited punishment. Claiming salvation without evidence is rejected:

“That is because they say: ‘The Fire will never touch us except for a number of days.’ And what they used to invent deceived them regarding their religion.” (Q 3:24)

The Qur’ān warns that such ideas are self-deception.

The Qur’ān does not teach guaranteed intercession. It repeatedly declares that no one — not even a prophet — can intercede unless God permits it, for whom He wills, and only according to truth, not as a favor.

The Qur’ān’s own testimony is that no soul can intercede for another:

“Fear a Day when no soul will avail another in anything, no intercession will be accepted…” (Q 2:48)

Intercession is only by God’s permission:

“Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?” (Q 2:255)

Even angels cannot intercede:

“How many angels in the heavens whose intercession avails nothing except after God permits it for whom He wills and approves.” (Q 53:26)

Intercession does not benefit those who reject truth:

“Intercession of the intercessors will not benefit them.” (Q 74:48)

No intercession can override justice:

“Say: Intercession is entirely with God.” (Q 39:44)

The Qur’ān leaves no room for the idea that Muhammad — or anyone else — can rescue sinners based on affiliation, identity, or words uttered without sincere righteousness.

The Qur’ān repeatedly describes Hell for the guilty as eternal, unless God Himself chooses otherwise (not due to any intercessor):

“…abiding therein forever.” (Q 4:169; 33:65; 72:23)

There is zero Qur’ānic support for an automatic release or prophetic override.

According to the Qur’ān:

No one can intercede except by God’s permission.

No one knows whom God will permit.

Intercession cannot override God’s justice.

False religious guarantees are condemned.

Reciting formulas offers no protection without righteousness.

Those condemned to Hell remain there eternally unless God wills otherwise — not because someone intervenes.

According to hadith-based theology:

Analogous to Christian theology where salvation is mediated through Jesus, Muhammad intercedes for sinful Muslims.

Anyone who recites the shahada will eventually leave Hell.

Prophetic favor overrides divine judgment.

These two positions cannot be reconciled.

## The Denigrative Framing of Women

It is mentioned in secondary hadith literature and attributed to Prophet Muhammad that women constitute the majority of Hell's inhabitants, are inherently deficient in intellect and religion, are an ill omen, and invalidate a man's prayer if they pass in front of him (alongside dogs and donkeys). Let’s look at a few examples:

#### Majority of women in hell

Sahih al-Bukhari 5197 / Sahih Muslim 2737:

Narrated by Ibn 'Abbas: The Prophet allegedly said: "I looked into Paradise and saw that the majority of its dwellers were the poor; and I looked into Hell and saw that the majority of its dwellers were women."

#### Deficient in Intellect and Religion

Sahih al-Bukhari 304 / Sahih Muslim 79:

Narrated by Abu Sa'id al-Khudri: The Prophet allegedly addressed a group of women, saying: "I have not seen anyone more deficient in intellect and religion who can sway the mind of a resolute man than one of you... Is not the testimony of a woman equal to half that of a man? That is from the deficiency of her intellect. And does she not abstain from prayer and fasting during menses? That is from the deficiency of her religion."

#### Equated to Dogs and Donkeys in Invalidating Prayer

Sahih Muslim 510a / Sunan an-Nasa'i 751:

Narrated by Abu Dharr: The Prophet allegedly said: "A Muslim's prayer is severed/invalidated if there is not in front of him something like the back of a saddle, by [the passage of] a woman, a donkey, and a black dog."

#### Prohibitions and Ill Omens

Sahih al-Bukhari 5093 / Sahih Muslim 2170:

Narrated by ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar: The Prophet said: "Bad luck or an ill omen is found in three things: in a woman, a house, and a horse. "

### Qur’anic Anchoring

Knowledge of who will inhabit Hell is a clear contradiction of "I do not know what will be done with me or with you" (Q 46:9) and "If I knew the unseen, I would have acquired much good" (Q 7:188). Claiming that the Prophet was shown the metaphysical realm of the Afterlife and counted the gender demographics of Hell represents a direct collapse of the Qurʾān's epistemological boundaries.

In Sūrat al-Ahzāb (Q 33:35), the Qurʾān outlines ten paired categories of spiritual virtue (e.g., al-muslimīn wa-l-muslimāt, al-mu'minīn wa-l-mu'mināt), applying the exact same standards and rewards to men and women without a single mention of biological or intellectual inferiority. Verse Q 4:124 establishes absolute, symmetric spiritual parity: "Whoever does righteous deeds, whether male or female, while being a believer—those will enter Paradise." Q 9:71 defines believing men and women as mutual protecting allies (awliyāʾ).

To claim that women possess an innate "deficiency in religion" due to natural, God-given biological cycles (menstruation) turns a divine biological design into a moral defect. There is no rule in the Qur’an that prevents women from their religious commitments when in a state of menstruation. The only command is to abstain from sexual intercourse during her periods which applies as much to men as it does to women (Q 2:222).  This is yet another example of secondary literature over riding the primary text by transforming a physical hygiene boundary into a moral/spiritual deficiency.

Equating women with dogs is completely antithetical to the Qur’anic spirit of spiritually treating both men and women equally. That we find Āʾishah’s rejection (Sahih al-Bukhari 511) provides further evidence of the kind of fabricated and contradictory hadith that exist in the corpus.

Categorizing women as inherently prone to "ill omens" directly conflicts with the Qurʾānic worldview, which explicitly rejects superstitious omen-seeking as a pagan practice (Q 7:131; 27:47; 36:18–19).

Said they: “We think you an evil omen. If you cease not, we will stone you; and there will touch you from us a painful punishment.” (Q 36:18)

It is ironic how the Hadith promotes content that is exclusively reserved in the Qur’an for pagans. In the verse above, not only are the messengers being equated with an evil omen, but also threatened with the punishment of stoning.

## Mandatory Obedience to Tyrants

Hadith instruct believers to submit completely to religious and political authorities without questioning their legitimacy, ethics, or actions.

Sahih Muslim 1847b (Kitab al-Imarah):

Narrated by Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman: The Prophet allegedly said: "There will be leaders who will not be guided by my guidance and who will not adopt my ways... You must listen to the leader and carry out his orders; even if your back is flogged and your wealth is seized, you must obey."

### Quranic Anchoring

This narration is contradicted by the Qur’an:

"And do not obey the command of the transgressors who spread corruption in the land and do not reform." (Q 26:151-152)

The Qurʾān mandates standing up for absolute justice (Q 5:8) and actively defending the oppressed (Q 4:75), whereas this Hadith demands total quietism and submission to tyrants who seize property and inflict physical violence.

From a systems-level historical perspective, the function of this report is transparent: it was forged ex post (after the fact) perhaps during the later Umayyad or Abbasid periods to legitimize royal transgressions, suppress political rebellion, and shield tyrannical state authority by retrofitting it with Prophetic mandate.

## Endorsement of  Child Marriage

While Hadith apologist claim to venerate Prophet Muhammad by following his Hadith and Sunnah, their beliefs and actions show the exact opposite. Many Hadith exist in the secondary corpus that denigrate his character. Polemicists accuse him of endorsing child marriage, citing hadith reports that claim he married ʿĀʾishah when she was six and consummated his marriage at nine. Such narratives are then used to portray both the Prophet and the Qur’an as condoning these practices.

These reports are in main hadith collections and include:

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī — 5133, 5134, 5158: Narrated ʿĀʾishah: that the Prophet (ﷺ) married her when she was six years old and he consummated his marriage when she was nine years old, and then she remained with him for nine years (i.e., till his death).

Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim — 1422: Narrated ʿĀʾishah: that the Prophet (ﷺ) married her when she was six years old and he consummated his marriage when she was nine years old, and then she remained with him for nine years (i.e., till his death).

Sunan an-Nasa’ī — 3378: It was narrated that 'Aishah said: "The Messenger of Allah married me when I was six, and consummated the marriage with me when I was nine, and I used to play with dolls.

Sunan Ibn Mājah — 1877: Includes a detailed account of ʿĀʾishah’s age, illness, playing on an urjuhah (swing/seesaw) with friends, and the marriage consummation at nine.

Musnad Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal — 24139, 24602, 24603, 25636: Reiterate the claim of ʿĀʾishah’s marriage at six and consummation at nine.

A related hadith in Sahih Muslim 1422c also mentions Āʾishah playing with dolls, stating: "I used to play with the dolls in the presence of the Prophet, and my girl friends also used to play with me".

Classical exegetes such as al-Tabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurtubī accepted these reports as historically reliable, and leaned on extra-Qur’anic sources to reinterpret Q 65:4 thereby normalizing the notion of prepubescent marriage.

### Quran Q 65:4: Wording and Grammar

Sūrah At-Talaq pertains to legal rulings pertaining to divorce. The first verse of this chapter makes it plain that the topic is a waiting period (iddah) for women after divorce. This is to make sure she is not pregnant with her former husband's child if she wants to marry again. Q 65:1-4 build on the waiting period after divorce mentioned earlier in Q 2:228. Verse Q 65:4 provides more details about the waiting period for divorced women before they can remarry:

"And as for those of your women (nisāʾ) who have despaired of menstruation, if you have a doubt, their prescribed time shall be three months; and [the same applies to] those who have not yet menstruated. As for those who are pregnant, their prescribed time is until they deliver their burdens…" (Q 65:4)

This verse shows important word and grammar clues. The key word is nisāʾ (نِسَاء), meaning “women” in Arabic. Its three-letter root nūn sīn wāw appears about 59 times in the Qur'an as only the noun nisā (نِسَاء) and always refers to adult females in legal matters like marriage, divorce, and inheritance (e.g., Q 4:3; 4:11; 4:127). The subject in the verse is nisāʾikum (“your women”), referring to adult female spouses. The female plural pronoun wallāʾī (وَاللَّائِي, “and those who”) points back to nisāʾ in the previous part, and the 3rd person plural verb yahidna (يَحِضْنَ, “have not menstruated”) is linked in grammar to nisāʾ, keeping adult women as the subject throughout the verse. So, based on the words and grammar, there is no reason to think the underlined part includes prepubescent girls; it clearly talks about adult women.

The phrase wallāʾī lam yahidna (“and those who have not yet menstruated”) can be seen as referring to two likely groups of adult women who may not menstruate:

Women past menopause, who have naturally stopped menstruating because of age.

Women with body conditions, such as amenorrhea caused by hormonal imbalances, stress, illness, or irregular cycles.

Pregnant women are mentioned separately in the same verse, and their waiting period (iddah) ends when they give birth. Importantly, the Qur’an does not use any Arabic terms like sabiyyah (young girl), jāriyah (maiden), or tiflah (child) when talking about females who can marry in this or any other place. The grammar and setting of Q 65:4, when read with related verses, always uses nisāʾ (women), a term for adults. Claims that this verse allows child marriage have no basis in the Qur’an’s words or grammar.

### Mainstream Interpretation of Q 65:4

Tafsīr commentators of the Qur'an explained Q 65:4 as applying not only to women past menopause but also to girls who have not started menstruating yet. Ibn Kathīr writes:

لِلصَّغِيرَةِ الَّتِي لَمْ تَحِضْ

(al-saghīrah allatī lam tahid — “the young girl who has not menstruated”).

He clearly includes girls before puberty in the verse's meaning, aligning with many earlier scholars like al-Tabarī and al-Qurtubī. To back this claim, Ibn Kathīr uses narrations, including widely mentioned hadith reports about ʿĀʾishah’s marriage in Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim and a report linked to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb:

“O Messenger of Allah, some women were not mentioned in the Book—namely the young, the old, and the pregnant. Later on, this Ayah was revealed (i.e Q 65:4)”

Exegetes put together verse Q 65:4 with the hadith of ʿĀʾishah and the report of Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, using them collectively as strong proof that the Qur’an allows marriage to young girls. Hence, these scholars provide hadith influenced context to the verse in their interpretations.

### Tafsīr Interpretation at Odds with Qur'an's Linguistic Analysis

The tafsīr interpretation doesn't match the Qur’an’s own linguistic patterns. The Qur’an never uses the term al-saghīrah (“the young girl”) when setting rules for marriage or divorce. It consistently uses the term nisāʾ (women). Moreover, if verse 65:4 was indeed revealed in response to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb’s question about the categories of females to which the waiting period applies, one would expect it to begin with the formula “They ask you…Say…” (yasʾalūnaka / yastaftūnaka…qul…), as in other clarifying verses including those pertaining to women’s issues (Q 2:222; 4:127; 4:176). Yet this is not the case: Q 65:4 begins with wa (“and”), a resumption particle indicating continuation from Q 64:1, which itself starts with yā ayyuhā al-nabiyu (“O Prophet”), showing that the divine guidelines on the waiting period after divorce were communicated directly to Muhammad without any prompting from his companions.

Furthermore, Kaʿb’s question, reportedly asked before Q 65:4 was revealed, assumes young girls as a category to which this verse applies in advance—an assumption later tafsīr commentators treated as authoritative. This reflects circular logic, or classic begging-the-question fallacy: the interpretation assumes the conclusion (that young girls are included) rather than letting the Qur’an’s words and grammar define the verse’s meaning. Such assumed interpretations distort the reading of the divine text, creating a tension between the Qur’an’s words and the main tafsīr tradition.

This demonstrates the Calibration axis in practice: when external claims are tested against the Qurʾān’s own meanings, patterns, grammar, and cross-referential structure, the Qurʾān filters out distortions and restores truth.

### Marriage in the Qur’an: Adulthood, Consent, and Maturity

The Qur'an Q 4:1 and Q 4:3-4, in Sūrah An-Nisa (The Women), further makes clear that marriage is for adult, mature women by using nisāʾ and stressing qualities linked to adulthood. The passage begins:

O mankind! Be conscious of your Sustainer, who has created you out of one living entity, and out of it created its mate, and out of the two spread abroad a multitude of men (rijāl) and women (nisāʾ). And remain conscious of God, in whose name you demand [your rights] from one another, and of these ties of kinship. Verily, God is ever watchful over you! (Q 4:1)

Here, nisāʾ denotes adult women, counterparts to men (rijāl), in the context of creation and familial bonds. Verses Q 4:3–4 continue:

"And if you have reason to fear that you might not act equitably towards orphans, then marry from among women (nisāʾ) such as are lawful to you – [even] two, or three, or four: but if you have reason to fear that you might not be able to treat them with equal fairness, then [only] one – or [from among] those whom you rightfully possess. This will make it more likely that you will not deviate from the right course. And give unto women their marriage portions in the spirit of a gift; but if they, of their own accord, give up unto you aught thereof, then enjoy it with pleasure and good cheer." (Q 4:3–4)

These verses use nisāʾ to describe marriageable women, emphasizing justice, consent, and financial responsibilities like dowers, which point to adult partners capable of mutual agreement and marital duties. The reference to orphans in Q 4:3 is linked to Q 4:6 which states:

And test the orphans [in your charge] until they reach the age of marriage (balaghū an-nikāh); then, if you find them to be mature of mind (rushd), hand over to them their possessions…” (Q 4:6)

The Qur'an requires waiting for orphans to reach "the age of marriage" (balaghū an-nikāh) before entrusting property and mental maturity (rushd), underscoring the principle that legal and social responsibility requires adulthood. Read holistically, 4:1–4 with 4:6 indicates that marriage is intended for adult women who have reached mental maturity, consistent with the Quran’s text and grammar.

The Quran’s marriage rulings consistently use nisāʾ to refer to marriageable females, as seen in verses like Q 2:221-223, Q 4:22–24, and Q 4:25, which address believing women capable of consent and responsibility. Verses Q 4:19 and Q 4:21 present marriage as a solemn covenant (mīthāqan ghalīzan) and emphasize mutual consent and kindness in marriage, indicating partners with the capacity for informed decision-making. The Qur'an’s silence on prepubescent marriage, combined with its emphasis on reaching the age of maturity (e.g., Q 4:6), confirms that marriage is between adult males and females.

The discussion in Q 2:222 assumes women in marital relations are menstruating, and of reproductive age. Q 24:58-59 establishes that children are subject to special privacy rules until they reach puberty (hulm)—an age of discernment and maturity—after which they are treated as adults responsible for observing household boundaries similar to those senior to them. Taken together, these verses reveal a consistent framework: marriage in the scripture is firmly grounded in adulthood, responsibility, and consent.

Hadith claiming ʿĀʾishah was a child bride and played with dolls or on a swing with friends conflict with the Quran’s consistent use of nisāʾ and its focus on adult females (e.g., Q 4:6; 4:19). The evidence demonstrates the Qur’an’s correspondence with empirical moral reality: marriage requires maturity, consent, and responsibility — conditions impossible for children — aligning the Qur'an with observable human nature rather than inherited cultural norms.
