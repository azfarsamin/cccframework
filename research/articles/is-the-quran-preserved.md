## Is the Qur’an Preserved?[^1]

## Introduction

As discussed earlier, our goal is to systematically develop a coherent belief system. The underlying approach is to find a text that fast-tracks our understanding of reality — find the box lid as opposed to completing the puzzle without it. In other words, we are interested in establishing truth and deciding based on a structured set of criteria — the CCC Framework — which book validates its claim of divinity with the least explanatory friction. A distorted message cannot serve as a reliable foundation for truth.

On the coherence axis, one key constraint is that the information we use to develop our core belief system must show internal textual consistency, for anyone can claim to possess the truth. The source—whether a single book or a library—must contain attributes that set it apart from competing doctrines and ideologies. In this chapter, we test the claim whether the Qurʾān is precisely such a source. Specifically, we evaluate a text making a preservation claim that appears to have resisted tampering, stood the test of time, and remains in its original language in both its textual and oral forms.

The CCC Framework is then applied to infer the Qurʾān’s revelation claim—not assume it—by testing its sustained performance across all three axes. If the evidence consistently converges, divine revelation becomes the explanation that best fits the evidence.

## **A Testable Claim of Preservation**

The Qurʾān makes a foundational claim of preservation that underpins all remaining claims:

> *“Indeed, We have sent down the Reminder, and We are surely its guardian.”* (Q 15:9)

Thus the Book, claiming to be from God, declares a testable preservation claim: revelation will remain safeguarded under divine guardianship.

A substantial body of manuscript, linguistic, and historical work suggests that the Qurʾān has remained structurally intact, semantically consistent, and conceptually unified despite fourteen centuries of transmission. Over this period, the claim of preservation is empirically testable from the available body of archaeological, paleographical and codicological evidence.

The Qurʾān was revealed in Arabic and preserved in its original language. Translation aids understanding but cannot replace the source text, as languages carry unique idioms, lexical idiosyncrasies, and conceptual nuances specific to classical Arabic. The preservation of linguistic form ensures recoverability of intended meaning—a key aspect of coherence.

A number of studies show that rhythmic symmetry, recurring phrases, and cross-referential coherence function as internal guardrails. Altering one part disrupts the semantic network of many others. Linguistic and structural integrity thus constitute the Qurʾān’s built-in preservation mechanism.[^2]

## A Distributed System of Preservation

The Qurʾān’s preservation operates through two synchronized media: written record and living memory. Millions worldwide continue to memorize the entire text verbatim—a remarkable phenomenon that, to date, has no parallel in human textual transmission. Even if every printed copy disappeared, the Qurʾān could eventually be reconstructed from collective memory, fulfilling the safeguard promised in Q 15:9.

The Qurʾān functions like a self-correcting network in which each memorizer acts as a node verifying every other. This resembles a tamper-resistant distributed ledger,[^3] but the Qurʾānic design predates such technology by centuries: a living system embedded in human memory rather than machines. Millions of *ḥuffāẓ* — serve as decentralized verifiers of the authentic text. Any deviation in recitation or script today can be immediately exposed through this global redundancy. Unlike a technological invention, this unique architecture relies on “network effects” as the mechanism of preservation sustained through memorization and continuous recitation.

This dual-channel calibration — oral + written transmission — is supported by the Qurʾān’s claim of ease of remembrance and a written tradition present from the beginning.

> *“And We have certainly made the Qurʾān easy for remembrance; so is there any who will remember?”* (Q 54:17)

Thus, every memorizer contributes to the fulfillment of the Qurʾān’s claim.

Preservation is therefore not static archiving, but an active, ongoing process of verification woven into ritual, recitation, and scholarship.

## Preservation and the Question of Multiple Recensions

This work recognizes that multiple recitational readings (*qirāʾāt*) of the Qurʾān exist today. Traditional reports about *aḥruf* (“modes” or “forms”)—compiled generations later—attempted to explain observable transmission differences through ḥadīth. From a Qurʾān-centric standpoint, however, these accounts represent post-revelatory rationalizations rather than the Qurʾān’s own testimony.

The Qurʾān itself affirms a single, safeguarded revelation, not a plurality of competing textual versions:

> *“Falsehood cannot approach it from before it or from behind it; a revelation from the Wise and Praiseworthy.”* (Q 41:42)

In early Islam, regional pronunciation and orthographic ambiguity in the unvoweled and undotted *rasm* naturally produced minor reading differences. Over time, these were formalized into canonical *qirāʾāt*, the two most widespread being *Ḥafṣ ʿan ʿĀṣim* (≈ 95 %) and *Warsh ʿan Nāfiʿ* (≈ 3 %).[^4] Thus, while more than one *muṣḥaf* edition exists today, such differences remain phonetic, grammatical or limited lexical differences within a shared semantic framework— they do not produce competing doctrines or theologies., a feature that stands out among ancient scriptures. The *Ḥafṣ ʿan ʿĀṣim* reading was later standardized for print in what is commonly referred to as the Cairo 1924 edition.

In contrast to earlier Abrahamic scriptures whose textual variants produced considerable doctrinal divergence, the Qurʾān’s *qirāʾāt* display a remarkably narrow range of non-theological variation within a preserved semantic core. A pan-textual, morphological, and grammatical analysis across recensions can therefore recover precise meaning, as demonstrated in **Appendix C**.

The major recitational systems—such as *Ḥafṣ* and *Warsh*—therefore reflect normal transcriptional or phonetic nuance within one stable consonantal framework, not distinct theologies or competing versions. This is precisely what it means for God’s promise in Q 15:9 to hold: endogenous recoverability of *meaning, guidance, structure, and message*. Not the fossilization of typography, and not the harmonization of later scholarly constructs like “seven *aḥruf*” or “ten *qirāʾāt.*”

## **Empirical Verification through Manuscripts**

Unlike other scriptures whose preservation depends on institutional control, the Qurʾān declares divine self-preservation (Q 15:9). This promise is unique because the Qurʾān is both the message and the object of protection—its very text being the medium of revelation. Archaeological manuscript evidence corroborates this stability:

### Topkapı Sarayı Medina 1a (M1) (8<sup>th</sup> – early 9<sup>th</sup> CE)

Originally preserved in the Prophet’s Mosque in Medina, it was moved to Istanbul during World War I. It is arguably the earliest surviving virtually complete physical copy of the Qur'an, comprising 391 folios written by multiple scribes. Its orthographic variants preserve evidence of early regional written transmission. Only a couple of later replacement folios exist (e.g., folios 24 and 33 in *Surah Āl ʿImrān*), while the original quires[^5] preserve the full text from *al-Fātiḥah* through *al-Nās*. Although it is not carbon dated, since physical destructive sampling has not been permitted by Turkish authorities, the dating has been established via paleography and codicology.[^6]

### Codex Mashhad (MS 18 & MS 4116) (Late 7<sup>th</sup> CE)

Dated palaeographically to the 1st century AH. Notably, it preserves evidence of an underlying surah arrangement that originally aligned with the order recorded for Abdallāh ibn Masʿūd, which was subsequently emended to conform to the standard ʿUthmānic surah order. The rearrangements were strictly at the macro-level of entire chapters (*surahs*), not individual verses (*āyāt*). Codex Mashhad demonstrates that the standard ʿUthmānic wording was already being reproduced in an alternative surah arrangement before the manuscript was reorganized to match the standard sequence. It consists of 251 folios preserving over 90% of the Qur'an.[^7]

### Samarkand (Tashkent) codex (Late 7<sup>th</sup> – early 9<sup>th</sup> CE)

Historically preserved in Samarkand, this manuscript is now housed in Tashkent, Uzbekistan.[^8] Radiocarbon testing and palaeographic analysis place its creation between the late 7th century and early 9th century CE, roughly a century after Caliph 'Uthman's death. It is a partial text (accounting for roughly 30–35% of the Quran) consisting of around 353 folios[^9] that begin midway through *Sūrah al-Baqarah* and extends to *sūrat l-zukh'ruf* with intervening gaps. Although there are lacunae due to lost leaves, its underlying consonantal text (the *rasm*) shows no meaningful textual divergence from the Qurʾān of today (Cairo 1924 edition).[^10]

### Topkapi (Istanbul) codex (Late 8<sup>th</sup> - early 9<sup>th</sup> CE) 

The Topkapi manuscript preserves **nearly the entire Qurʾānic text,** with only minor lacunae due to missing folios. Its consonantal framework belongs to the standard ʿUthmānic textual tradition. Although the manuscript contains scribal and orthographic differences, along with some documented textual variants, it preserves remarkably close correspondence with the Qur'an transmitted today. It **does *not* diverge in meaning** from the standard Qurʾān recited today supporting the claim of long-term semantic stability.[^11]

### Codex Parisino-petropolitanus (Late 7<sup>th</sup> – early 8<sup>th</sup> CE)

The **Codex Parisino-petropolitanu**s is one of the oldest surviving manuscripts of the Qur’an, dating back to the late 7th to early 8th century CE (the Umayyad period). The surviving dispersed folios account for roughly 45% of the Qur'anic text. It was written by five scribes on parchment in the *Ḥijāzī* script—an early, slanted Arabic script characteristically lacking vowels and diacritical markings. It is remarkably close to the modern standard text of the Quran, with the underlying consonantal text (the *rasm*) verified to be virtually identical to the standard 'Uthmanic tradition used today, differing only in minor surface details due to early orthography, scribal errors, and regional variant readings (*qirāʾāt*).[^12]

### Ṣanʿāʾ Collection (7<sup>th</sup> – early 8<sup>th</sup> CE)

The **Ṣanʿāʾ cache** discovered during restoration of the Great Mosque of Ṣanʿāʾ in 1972 contained thousands of Qur'anic manuscript fragments. The famous **Ṣanʿāʾ 1 / DAM 01-27.1**[^13] used in textual-critical arguments is one particular manuscript that has generated significant interest in recent years. It is a palimpsest[^14] and studies show that the parchment dates to the 6-7th century CE with two textual layers:[^15]

- **Ṣanʿāʾ 1 lower text (C-1 / scriptio inferior)**

- Earlier Qur'anic writing on the parchment — later erased

- Date Range: 578–669 CE (at a 95% confidence interval)

- Significantly non-standard textual profile

- **Ṣanʿāʾ 1 upper text (scriptio superior)**

- Subsequently written over the erased parchment

- Date Range: 7<sup>th</sup> - early 8th century CE

- Belongs to the standard consonantal Qur'anic tradition.

It is the lower text which has generated a lot of controversy in recent times. Polemicists have used findings from recent historical critical scholarship to make an inference that the standard Qur’an possessed today evolved from an earlier text. But here we are only interested in what the actual evidence shows.

If Ṣanʿāʾ preserves an earlier developmental Qur'an, we should find some coherent direction that would provide evidence of evolution such as theological clarification, legal development, cancellation and replacement of command, or anything that would tell us that the Qur’an was in a state of evolution. Here are the scholarly findings on a higher level:

- Sadeghi and Goudarzi concluded that the lower text and the standard Qur'an generally contain the same verses within the same chapters (*sūrahs*), demonstrating that the *sūrahs* were already assembled before the two textual traditions diverged. Both the **lower (L)** and **upper (U)** texts had a common ancestor (the **Prophet’s prototype, or** **P**).

- With two exceptions—the omission of Q 9:85, attributed to an apparent scribal eye-skip, and the reversal of the short verses Q 20:31–32—the surviving verses generally appear in their expected positions.[^16]

- There is variation in the order of chapters as well as in the wording within chapters.

It is the last point that we need to unpack at a more granular level to understand variation from a preservation vantage point and see if there is any evidence of chronological evolution and textual displacement.

#### Evidence of Auto-Contamination

When a directionality test is run, there is no coherent editorial evolution from Ṣanʿāʾ lower → standard Qur’an. Instead, we note a noisy-copy/transmission profile. When the variants are analyzed, it is a case of semantic noise when an already highly structured, repetitive text is reproduced imperfectly in a written and oral transmission milieu. Sadeghi identifies auto-contamination as one of the mechanisms — wording from elsewhere in the Qurʾān interfering with the scribe’s reproduction of the text from memory.[^17]

Their conclusion is not that Ṣanʿāʾ preserves some radically different Qur'an. They say the opposite: **Ṣanʿāʾ L and the standard Qur'an are generally the same and the differences are the exception rather than the rule. More importantly, they argue that the two texts are sister branches from a common source (**$\mathbf{P}$**)—** **not that L serves as an ancestral prototype.**

#### Evidence of Explanatory Contamination

In certain cases, such as Q 19:26, there is additional wording that cannot be explained through auto-contamination. Here is the verse from the standard reading:

> <span dir="rtl">فَكُلِى وَٱشْرَبِى وَقَرِّى عَيْنًا فَإِمَّا تَرَيِنَّ مِنَ ٱلْبَشَرِ أَحَدًا فَقُولِىٓ إِنِّى نَذَرْتُ لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ صَوْمًا فَلَنْ أُكَلِّمَ ٱلْيَوْمَ إِنسِيًّا</span>
>
> *“So eat thou, and drink thou, and let thine eye be comforted; and if thou see any mortal, say thou: ‘I have vowed to the Almighty a fast (sawman), so I will not speak this day to any man.’” (Q 19:26)*

The reported additional word *wa-ṣamtan* (“and silence”) is added in Sanʿāʾ L after *sawman* and lacks a corresponding expression elsewhere in the standard Qur'an. Marijn van Putten argues that the longer wording is more original because it cannot be explained through auto-contamination from another Qur'anic passage.[^18] But an alternate explanation could be that it is “explanatory contamination” for the remainder of the verse “*so I will not speak this day to any man*” as opposed to the claim that standardized Qur'an lost an original word. Van Putten’s conclusion posits a visual omission while excluding auto-contamination, but does not eliminate explanatory contamination arising from the verse's own wording.

To support his thesis, van Putten invokes retrospective literary attributions to Anas ibn Malik and Ibn Masud to argue that the longer reading in the Ṣanʿāʾ L preserves the original wording. He cites Ibn Khalawayh (d. 980 CE) for a reading attributed to Anas, and Abu Hayyan (d. 1344 CE) for an attribution to Ibn Masud's codex. But his treatment of the traditional evidence is a textbook example of cherry-picking.

Consider what he leaves out. Al-Tabari (d. 923 CE), writing before both authorities cited by van Putten, preserves multiple reports from Anas ibn Malik and Ibn Abbas explicitly explaining *sawman* (fasting) as *samtan* (silence). In the very same collection, one report attributes the longer reading *sawman wa-samtan* (fasting and silence) to Anas, while reports from Qatada describe *samtan* as an alternative reading rather than an addition.[^19]

The evidence presents three different treatments of the disputed word:

- Explanation — three reports explaining *ṣawman* as *ṣamtan*

- Substitution — reports of *ṣamtan* as an alternative reading

- Addition — one report of *ṣawman wa-ṣamtan*

Yet van Putten privileges the addition as evidence of original wording without establishing why the others should be discounted.

The irony is difficult to miss. Al-Tabari introduces these reports as explanations of the meaning of *sawm*. The very exegetical tradition invoked to corroborate the longer reading therefore provides positive evidence for explanatory contamination. The additional word *samtan* merely repeats what the existing verse already communicates: *"so I will not speak this day to any man."*

Van Putten argues that the longer reading cannot have arisen through auto-contamination because the additional word has no corresponding expression elsewhere in the Qur'an. He then concludes that the standard Qur'an must have lost an original word through scribal omission. **This is a non sequitur. Eliminating one mechanism of addition does not establish omission.** His argument fails to eliminate the possibility that an explanatory gloss became incorporated into L—precisely the kind of explanation suggested by al-Tabari's reports.

His appeal to Ibn Masud's alleged codex compounds the problem. Abu Hayyan makes the attribution approximately seven centuries after Ibn Masud. Where is the manuscript? No authenticated copy of Ibn Masud's codex survives. Moreover, the reported reading *samtan* is not even identical to the longer *sawman wa-samtan* formulation, since substitution is not the same as addition.

Abū Ḥayyān writes:

> <span dir="rtl">وفي مصحف عبد الله صمتاً</span>
>
> “*And in the codex of ʿAbd Allāh \[Ibn Masʿūd\]:* *ṣamtan*.”

Van Putten is not only using evidence selectively to build the argument of a longer reading but authenticating an addition with a substitution.

What he has established is that the Ṣanʿāʾ L contains a disputed additional expression and that similar readings appear in much later literary reports. He has not established that the additional word belonged to the original Qur'an, that the standard text lost it, or that the supposed companion codices independently corroborate its originality. The conclusion simply does not follow.

Q 19:26 is not the only example of an apparent explanatory addition. Sinai identifies approximately 18 additions of one or more words in the lower text compared to only six identified in the standard text in Nicolai Sinai's 2020 reassessment.[^20] At Q 2:91, for example, the Ṣanʿāʾ L expands the standard wording, “We believe in what has been sent down to us,” with *min kutubin* (“of scriptures”). Nicolai Sinai acknowledges the explanatory character of this addition, noting that variants of this type function naturally as scribal glosses. This provides another concrete example of how additional wording can arise without establishing that the standard Qur'an lost an original expression.

See Table D.3 in Appendix D for examples of additions.

Thus, the existence of a L reading does not establish that the standard Qur'an lost original wording, much less demonstrate a process of textual evolution. The claim that L didn’t descend from the ʿUthmānic *rasm* doesn’t establish the reverse — that L is an ancestor. It requires additional evidence which an erased L does not deliver.

Elevating an erased manuscript reading into an original text by selectively invoking centuries-later reports, while overlooking the explanatory evidence preserved in an earlier exegetical source, is a methodological failure. The burden is on those claiming the longer reading was original to prove it.

#### Interpolation Hypothesis of Q 19:34-40 

Interestingly, a longstanding theory in Western scholarship—reiterated by van Putten—asserts that Q 19:34–40 are later additions on the basis of a literary hypothesis: that these seven verses disrupt the otherwise rhythmical structure and narrative flow while introducing a distinct anti-Christian polemical tone. This thesis is directly undermined by the presence of this verse block in sanʿāʾ L. It tells us that the lower text and the standard Qur’an of today had a common earlier ancestor. Van Putten explicitly acknowledges that the passage exists in both textual forms but then doubles down by speculating that the alleged insertion occurred before their divergence, possibly by Muhammad himself.[^21]

But where is the physical evidence of an earlier Qur'an without these verses? No such manuscript exists. And what evidence establishes Muhammad's supposed motive for inserting this passage when its language and message are already embedded throughout the Qur'an? Q 19:35 repeats the argument of Q 2:116–117; Q 19:36–37 closely parallels Q 43:64–65; and the rejection of divine sonship reappears emphatically in the same *sūrah* at Q 19:88–92. These are not isolated theological coincidences but examples of the Qur'an's self-similar textual architecture.

Thus, the scholarly interpolation thesis remains a *non sequitur* — literary speculation without manuscript corroboration.

**The reality is this**: the erased L manuscript evidence establishes the antiquity of these verses, not their interpolation. Western scholarship assumes the very proposition it needs to prove: that literary variation within a *sūrah* is evidence of a later insertion.

Thus, sanʿāʾ L has no demonstrated continuing manuscript lineage, and independent early codices corroborate the standard *rasm*. There is no evidence of an evolving Qur’an similar to what we find in the textual genealogies of Judeo-Christian scriptures. **There is no older theology being replaced, no legal development emerges, no doctrinal correction appears and no competing Qur'an evolves from the lower text.**

Ṣanʿāʾ manuscript points in the opposite direction: evidence of early internal composition of *sūrahs* and that the Qur’anic material was already circulating when L was written.

### Birmingham Manuscript (UK)

The Birmingham Manuscript (UK),[^22] radiocarbon dated to 568–645 CE (95.4% probability), is a Mingana 1572a parchment, which means it overlaps with the Prophet’s lifetime. While the dating applies to the writing material rather than the ink, the manuscript nonetheless constitutes one of the earliest extant witnesses to the Qurʾānic text. The specimen contains a very small fragment consisting of 2 folios (parts of *sūrahs* 18, 19, 20) and matches the standard Qurʾānic text at the consonantal level. The manuscript's extraordinary radiocarbon result became public in 2015 and recent research points to it being linked to the Paris BnF Arabe 328c.

### Paris BnF Arabe 328c (France)

The Paris BnF Arabe 328c is a crucial 1st-century Hijri (7th century CE) Qurʾānic manuscript fragment acquired from Egypt by a French collector in the 19th century. It is written on parchment and features minimal diacritical dots, and no vowel marks. While closely associated with the overall BnF Arabe 328 collection (which includes the Codex Parisino-Petropolitanus, shelfmark 328a), Arabe 328c represents a distinct, separate manuscript codex.

Codicological analysis by scholar Alba Fedeli demonstrated that the 16 folios of BnF Arabe 328c belong to the exact same original codex as the two famous folios of the Birmingham Quran manuscript (Mingana 1572a). Because it is part of the same manuscript as the Birmingham leaves, radiocarbon dating applies to the combined manuscript makes this one of the oldest surviving physical fragments of the Quran in the world. Together, the sixteen folios in Paris and two folios in Birmingham (18 folios total) preserve roughly 8.3% of the Quranic text. They are virtually identical to the standard printed Qur’an used today with no major deviations, structural changes, or missing passages.

And this is where the archaeology becomes difficult for the polemical narrative. The standard consonantal Qur'an is not something that suddenly appears after Ṣanʿāʾ lower. **It is independently sitting in an extremely early codex represented by Birmingham/Paris**, covering hundreds of verses across eight sūrahs.

### Evidence from Stemmatics Work

Marijn van Putten's stemmatics work — the idea that if two manuscripts share unique, distinctive peculiarities or non-standard spelling choices, they likely share a common ancestral copy — shows that early standard manuscripts point back **to a single written archetype**. In his 2019 paper, *The Grace of God*, he examined distinctive orthographic patterns across fourteen early Qur'anic manuscripts, including the Ṣanʿāʾ upper text. Birmingham/Paris/ Topkapi/San’a 1 (upper)/Samarkand belong to that family. If early regional codices (from Kufa, Basra, Damascus, and Medina) had been produced independently from oral memory or local dictation without a unified master copy, scribes would have written these words according to standard local phonetic spelling rules. Instead, van Putten provided overwhelming empirical evidence that early manuscripts across the Islamic world reproduce these **exact same non-phonetic spelling quirks in the exact same verse locations**.[^23]

**Example:**

Van Putten tracked a specific grammatical phrase: ***Niʿmat Allāh*** ("The Grace/Blessing of God"). The word *niʿmat* occurs repeatedly in the Qurʾān but is written in two different ways—sometimes with an open *tāʾ* and sometimes with the alternative feminine ending called *tāʾ marbūṭah*. There is no linguistic, phonetic, or dialectal reason for this switch. It is a completely arbitrary scribal quirk.

When van Putten orthographically analyzed 14 of the oldest surviving Quranic manuscripts found thousands of miles apart (including the famous Birmingham fragments, the Parisino-petropolitanus, and the Ṣanʿāʾ upper text), he found that **every single manuscript, where the passages survive, switches to the open *tāʾ* (<span dir="rtl">ت</span>) in the exact same verses, and stays with the closed *tāʾ* in the others**.

Non-phonetic spelling idiosyncrasies found at the exact same locations are not evidence of evolving textual variants; they are evidence of **an early physical, written copy** from which later standard manuscripts descended.

## Summary of Manuscript Evidence

The evidence overwhelmingly supports a common written Qur'anic archetype. There are multiple early manuscripts—with Birmingham/Paris chief among them—that share the same basic *rasm* as today's Qur'an. Alongside that dominant tradition, we find the highly unusual lower layer of the Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest—a witness with variant readings that was later erased and overwritten with the standard text.

Humans make mistakes. Birmingham/Paris itself contains scribal errors and corrections. None of that falsifies preservation unless the corrupted reading enters the transmitted textual lineage—as we saw in the Judeo-Christian traditions analyzed earlier. And Ṣanʿāʾ 1 Lower presents an extraordinary physical fact: it never did. It was erased. No textual genealogy evidence exists for a separate lineage.

When the Qurʾān of today (Cairo 1924 edition[^24]) is compared with its earliest surviving manuscripts, the observed differences are predominantly orthographic, with limited early lexical or word-order variation. The manuscripts, discovered in widely separated regions, converge on a common consonantal framework and textual structure—demonstrating that no regional recension ever displaced the unified Qurʾānic form. Dozens of additional first-century AH fragments across Europe and the Middle East corroborate this picture.

Broadly across modern Qurʾānic studies—without theological presupposition—scholars recognize this authorial coherence as distinctive. Islamic scholars emphasize the Qurʾān’s remarkable unity of voice and style, noting the absence of competing prophetic attributions or shifts in authorial persona.

## Structural Convergence of Evidence

Both independent transmission lines—written and oral—converge on the same corpus preserved in the Arabic language. This dual convergence satisfies what may be termed the **Functional Integrity Test:**[^28] a text remains preserved when both channels of transmission yield a consistent message, even in the presence of scribal drift or phonetic noise.

> *If T₁ (written), T₂ (oral) → convergence on the same Arabic corpus ⇒ preservation corroborated.*

Redundant convergence across transmission modes constitutes a calibrated design for redundancy and verification. Hence, the Qurʾān’s preservation is functional, from which meaning can be gleaned on a pan-textual basis. Meaningful preservation lies in the persistence of the same message under conditions of redundancy—more plausibly explained by design rather than chance.

> *“Indeed, We have sent down the Reminder, and We are surely its guardian.”* (Q 15:9)

This verse is fulfilled not in static ink but in a living, self-correcting network—millions of memorizers, thousands of manuscripts, and one enduring message. Within the CCC framework, preservation operates across three calibrated layers as shown in table 3.2.

Historically and empirically, this claim can now be tested and corroborated. The Qurʾān’s text has remained consistent from the earliest surviving manuscripts of the 7th century CE to modern printed editions. Oral transmission never lapsed: today, reciters from Nigeria to Indonesia and Morocco to Bosnia articulate wording, rhythm, and phonetic rules (*tajwīd*) with remarkable uniformity.[^29]

## Conclusion

The Qurʾān uniquely aligns its internal claim of preservation (Q 15:9) with observable reality: a stable linguistic form, continuous manuscript convergence toward a single original source, mass memorization, and uninterrupted liturgical use in its original language. This convergence constitutes a direct correspondence fit—a fulfilled, testable preservation claim sustained across fourteen centuries.

Modern scholarship demonstrates that the Qurʾānic text lacks the multi-recensional fragmentation, competing editions, and doctrinally divergent textual families characteristic of other scriptures.

Across more than fourteen centuries, the Qurʾān exhibits:

- one continuous speaker, one linguistic medium, and one internally consistent message,

- no anonymous or pseudonymous expansions,

- no rival authorial traditions or doctrinal textual families.

To falsify this preservation claim, the evidence must provide a **Qurʾānic equivalent** of:

- competing Hebrew textual families (e.g., Masoretic Text, Samaritan Pentateuch, and LXX Vorlage),

- composite Pentateuchal traditions falsely attributed to a single author,

- a Jewish or Christian canon reliant on extensive pseudonymous and anonymous authorship.

An isolated variant is evidence of variation, not proof of a competing textual genealogy. The Ṣanʿāʾ lower text is a case in point. Its distinctive readings have no demonstrated continuing manuscript lineage, while its extensive agreement with the standard Qurʾān independently corroborates substantial textual material predating the divergence of the traditions. The presence of orthographic and scribal errors in any religious text is also not proof of non-preservation but a testament to human fallibility. No single datum proves divinity, but each confirmed pattern of coherence raises the confidence of the Qurʾān’s claim. The Qurʾānic preservation hypothesis must therefore be tested against the totality of the surviving evidence, not defeated by selecting one anomalous manuscript and disregarding the broader transmission record.

By abductive reasoning and Occam’s Razor, the most economical explanation of this convergence of evidence is that, as per its claim, the Qurʾān represents preserved divine discourse rather than the product of cumulative human authorship. Contrary to prior Judeo-Christian scriptures, its message remains free of later alterations and singular: **one God, one revelation, and one moral law**.

**The falsification challenge is straightforward: produce an ancient text of comparable scale whose cumulative early manuscript record demonstrates superior temporal proximity, textual coverage, genealogical coherence and textual uniformity, with less explanatory friction.**

The Qur’an itself invites the reader to bring a Book that would overturn the claim of comparative evidentiary superiority:

> *Say thou: “Then bring a Book from God that gives better guidance than these:*[^30] *I will follow it, if you be truthful.” (Q 28:49)*

With the preservation of the Qur’an established, we now turn to the post-Qurʾānic literature—including *ḥadīth*, *sīrah*, and exegetical traditions. Does it meet the minimum preservation and coherence requirements? We tackle these questions in the next chapter.
