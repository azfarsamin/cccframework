# Surah Al-Shams (The Sun): The Self and the Balance of Creation
## When the Self Loses Its Balance: Lessons from Surah Al-Shams

## Introduction
Surah ash-Shams (The Sun) is one of the most beautifully structured short chapters of the Qurʾān. It begins with oaths describing the cosmic order — the sun, the moon, the sky, and the earth — then turns inward to speak about the human self (nafs).1
It teaches that the same balance and order that exist in the universe also exist within each human being. And when that balance is disturbed, the end result is cataclysmic, ending in total destruction — as seen in the case of the people of Thamūd, who received divine punishment for their transgression.

## Q 91:1–6 : The World in Perfect Balance
Wa-sh-shamsi wa-ḍuḥāhā

By the sun and its brightness

Wa-l-qamari idhā talāhā

and the moon when it follows it,

Wa-n-nahāri idhā jallāhā

and the day when it displays it,

Wa-l-layli idhā yaghshāhā

and the night when it covers it,

Wa-s-samāʾi wa mā banāhā

and the sky and Him who built it,

Wa-l-arḍi wa mā ṭaḥāhā

and the earth and Him who spread it.

Each oath points to something visible — the sun, moon, day, night, sky, and earth — to remind us that the whole world runs in perfect order.

The sun becomes visible and with it the morning light.

The moon follows its path in harmony.

Day reveals the sun and night covers it.

The sky is raised and built.

The earth is spread and made stable.

Everything has been created with purpose and works together with measure and precision. These opening verses show the outer balance of creation.

## Q 91:7–8 : The Self in Perfect Balance
Wa-nafsin wa mā sawwāhā

And by the self and Him who proportioned it,

Fa-alhamahā fujūrahā wa taqwāhā

and inspired it with its wickedness and its God-consciousness.

Just like the universe, the self (nafs) is shaped with care. God made it balanced — with the ability to distinguish right from wrong.

The word sawwāhā means to proportion, to make even, to balance perfectly.

Alhamahā means to inspire or implant awareness. It provides the moral polarity for discernment. Every living man and woman has an inner moral sense of both wrong (fujūr) and right (taqwā). Our task is to listen to that inner voice and choose rightly.

## Q 91:9–10 : The Great Test
Qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā

He has succeeded who purifies it,

Wa qad khāba man dassāhā

and he has failed who buries it.

These verses describe human choice. The verb zakkāhā means to purify, to nurture, to help grow. It refers to the morally accountable person who protects the nafs and actively makes an effort to develop it.

The word dassāhā means to bury or conceal. It describes the one who hides the self’s light under wrongdoing and heedlessness.

The Qurʾān makes it clear: every person decides. Whoever purifies the self succeeds;
whoever buries it fails.

## Q 91:11–14 : The Example of Thamūd
Kaththabat Thamūdu bi-ṭagwāhā

Thamūd denied because of its tyranny,

Idhi inbaʿatha ashqāhā

when its most wretched one rose up.

Fa-qāla lahum rasūlu Llāhi nāqata Llāhi wa suqyāhā

Then the messenger of God said to them: “The she-camel of God and her allotted watering!”

Fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-damdama ʿalayhim rabbuhum bi-dhanbihim fa-sawwāhā

But they denied him and hamstrung her, so their Lord crushed them for their sin and levelled it.

These verses are a parenthetical example placed within the surah to illustrate the moral law just described. In Q 91:7–10, the Qurʾān speaks about the self being shaped, inspired, and tested — whether it would choose purity or corruption. Now, verses Q 11–14 show that same test unfolding in a real community.

The people of Thamūd received a clear sign but rejected it out of arrogance.
Their refusal to honor watering rights (Q 54:28) and violence against the she-camel symbolized the complete burying of conscience. They acted without fear or restraint — just like the soul that covers up its awareness of right and wrong.

There is also a striking linguistic contrast here.
The same verb root س و ي (s-w-y) appears in both verse 7 and verse 14, but with opposite meanings:

In Q 91:7, sawwāhā — “He proportioned it” — describes creative harmony when God shapes the soul.

In Q 91 : 14, fa-sawwāhā — “He levelled it” — describes destructive re-balancing when God restores order through judgment.

The One who creates balance also restores balance when it is broken.
Thus, Thamūd’s destruction is not a digression but a demonstration of the same moral law in action.

## Q 91:15 : The End of the Story
Wa lā yakhāfu ʿuqbāhā

And He does not fear its consequence.

The last verse of the surah closes the loop with Q 91:10, after the conclusion of the parenthetical story of the people of Thamūd.2

The Arabic verb yakhāfu (“fear”) is imperfect, describing a continuing state. Read together with Q 91:10, it reflects the condition of the buried nafs in the present — the person who lives unafraid of the outcome of his deeds. This is not a single moment of defiance but a lasting state of heedlessness.

When conscience is buried, the heart no longer trembles at wrongdoing. Such a person moves through life without fear of consequence — the final stage of moral decay.

## Bringing It All Together
First, the Qurʾān shows the outer balance — the sun, moon, sky, and earth.
Next, it reveals the inner balance — the self
Then comes the test — will the human being purify or bury that self?
The story of Thamūd is the example of a people who chose corruption.

Finally, the surah ends by returning to the state of the buried self, which no longer fears consequence.

Everything in creation begins and ends with balance. When the self stays pure, life aligns with the harmony of creation. When the self is buried, disorder and blindness follow.

## Ring Composition (Concentric Symmetry)
The sūrah exhibits a clear concentric structure3 organized around a moral pivot:

A – Cosmic order (oaths by the sun, moon, day, night, sky, earth) (Q 91:1–6)

B – Inner order: the soul proportioned and morally inspired (Q 91:7–8)

C – Central pivot: success through purification vs failure through corruption (Q 91:9–10)

B′ – Collective moral failure: Thamūd as a historical instantiation of the same law (Q 91:11–14)

A′ – Moral outcome: absence of fear of consequence after corruption (Q 91:15)

The functional-level symmetry between A and A′ can be understood by applying our understanding of amānah (trust) in Q 33:72. Creation follows God’s order by necessity, while the human being—having accepted the amānah—is judged for violating it. Moreover, the historical example of Thamūd is not a digression; it mirrors the moral law articulated at the center, completing the ring.

## Reflection
The sun and moon show outer order.

The self shows inner order.

The same Creator designed both with perfect proportion.

The key verbs of this surah trace the journey of the soul:

sawwāhā — He balanced it.

alhamahā — He inspired it.

zakkāhā — the human purified it.

dassāhā — the human buried it.

Each of us carries the ability to reason morally and to distinguish between light and shadow within. This capacity is grounded in the human being’s original orientation toward truth (fiṭrah, Q 30:30) and moral accountability before God, affirmed in the primordial covenant (Q 7:172). According to Q 91:8, God endowed the human self with moral discernment through ilhām—awareness of both deviation and restraint. Success, according to the Qurʾān, depends on our conscious decision to strengthen the self, not to bury it.

Another layer of beauty in Surah ash-Shams lies in its sound and is a testament to the Qur’an’s poetic precision. Every verse in the surah ends with the same feminine pronoun suffix –hā (ـهَا) — fifteen times in total. This recurring ending ties together the sun, the moon, the day, the night, the sky, the earth, and the nafs — as if all creation and conscience return to the same source. The echo of –hā gives the surah a rhythmic unity that mirrors its message of balance and interconnectedness.

It is a perfect example of how the Qurʾān weaves meaning and sound into one continuous form of beauty.

## Closing Thought
The God who built the sky and spread the earth has also proportioned the self with the same precision. The heavens above and the heart within follow the same harmony in design by a single Creator. The task of life is to stay in balance with that design — by serving God alone and seeking His divine guidance to protect the light He placed within us.

1
Nafs in the Qurʾān denotes the human self as a morally exposed dimension whose role is to exert moral pressure on insān (human) to act (Q 12:53; 20:96; 50:16). The Qurʾān does not define the nafs as a detachable metaphysical soul distinct from the embodied person but treats it functionally as the human self in moral tension.
