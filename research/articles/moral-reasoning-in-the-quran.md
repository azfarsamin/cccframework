Moral Reasoning in the Qurʾān
A Pan-Textual Architecture of Reflection, Perception, and Human Judgement


Modern science treats the heart as a pump and the brain as the seat of thought. The Qurʾān locates moral reasoning in the heart — not as physiology, but as the center of human accountability and responsibility. The Qurʾān employs multiple internal faculties to describe how human beings perceive, process, and respond to reality. Among the most misunderstood terms are fuʾād (فؤاد) and qalb (قلب). Both are frequently conflated and translated loosely as “heart” in mainstream English renderings. A pan-textual reading, however, reveals that the Qurʾān consistently differentiates between these two terms, assigning each a distinct functional and moral role.

Rather than offering anatomical instruction or scientific harmonization to appeal to the modern secular mind, the Qurʾān presents a coherent anthropology grounded in human perception, intellectual awareness, and moral responsibility.

This article examines how fuʾād and qalb operate across the text, how their distinction becomes explicit in the account of Moses’ mother, and how the Qurʾān intentionally anchors moral reasoning—and the criteria that determine true success for a believer—in the heart (qalb).

Fuʾād as the Faculty of Inner Perceptive Awareness
Across the Qurʾān, fuʾād appears sixteen times. It is derived from the triliteral root ف أ د (f-ʾ-d), whose original meaning in the earliest Arabic lexicons is strikingly concrete. Lane's Arabic-English Lexicon, drawing on authorities such as Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs, defines the verb faʾada as "to roast," "to broil," or "to bake over a fire," especially flesh.

In the Qurʾān, it is almost exclusively alongside hearing (samʿ) and seeing (abṣār):

“He produced for you hearing, sight, and afʾidah — little are you grateful.” (Q 16:78)

“Indeed the hearing, the sight, and the afʾidah — all will be questioned.” (Q 17:36)

“And it is He who produced for you hearing and sight and afʾidah.” (Q 23:78)

“It is He who has produced you and made for you hearing and vision and afʾidah; little are you grateful.” (Q 67:23)

Modern translations tend to treat afʾidah, the plural of fuʾād, as an anatomical organ. However, the text presents a strict structural pattern:

Hearing (Samʿ) = Perceptive Faculty

Sight (Abṣār) = Perceptive Faculty

To translate afʾidah as a physical organ (”hearts”) in this repeated triad introduces a severe category error. To maintain internal structural consistency, the third item must represent an internal faculty of perception that processes what the first two gateways gather.

Historically, Arabic fuʾād poetically means the center of intense emotion where its usage is consistently preferred over qalb whenever emotion reaches an overwhelming intensity. Lovers speak of their fuʾād burning with longing after separation; poets describe the iḥtirāq al-fuʾād ("the burning of the fuʾād") in the anguish of bereavement; and warriors portray the fuʾād remaining steadfast amid the terror of battle. Thus, the original semantic picture is not simply "heart," but the burning center of the human being—the place where powerful emotions, convictions, and profound perceptions are experienced.

The Qurʾān, on the other hand, is not writing poetry — it’s repurposing this linguistic imagery from contemporary poetry and constructing functional epistemology.1

This repeated triad (hearing (samʿ) — seeing (abṣār) — afʾidah) establishes afʾidah as part of the perceptual architecture of human awareness. Hearing and seeing are sensory gateways; afʾidah functions as the inner conscious faculty that receives, experiences, and integrates perception. Let’s look at the following verse:

The fuʾādu did not lie about what it saw. (Q 53:11)

Hearts do not see literally. There is a level of seeing beyond sensory eyesight — an inner perceptive awareness that apprehends experience. The eyes may perceive externally but the fuʾād perceives internally and confirms the experience. Or in other words, it grounds the inductive process of generalizing by giving it meaning from human observation.

The role of afʾidah is thus experiential — the inner conscious awareness that experiences, perceives, reacts, and confirms reality. The etymology preserved in the classical lexicons and the usage found in early Arabic poetry therefore converge to portray the fuʾād as the heart understood in its most intense sense: the burning center of consciousness, emotion, and inward certainty. Translating afʾidah as “hearts” to express an anatomical organ alongside hearing and seeing is a category error when read pan-textually.

Distinct States of Afʾidah
There are three distinct states of the fuʾād identified in the Qur’an:

1. Operational / Calibrated
It is truth-aligned, aware, processing, and grounded (Q 11:120).

“And all We relate to thee from the reports of the messengers is that whereby We make firm (thabāt) thy fuʾād. And there has come to thee in this the truth, and an admonition, and a reminder for the believers.” (Q 11:120)

Through an inductive process, these narratives are conveyed in order to stabilize inner awareness.

2. Collapsed (Acute)
It is overwhelmed, empty, and non-functional (Q 14:43).

“Straining forward, their heads uplifted; their gaze not returning to them, and their afʾidah void (hawāʾ).” (Q 14:43)

This is vivid imagery of the hereafter depicting extreme psychological disorientation where the eyes can see (Q 14:42) but the inner perceptual awareness is entirely empty. It describes cognitive and experiential collapse under terror—consciousness without comprehension. Treating fuʾād as a physical heart here would render this verse entirely meaningless.

3. Withdrawn / Numbed (Chronic)
It is present but suppressed and disengaged, as in the case of the people of ʿĀd (Q 46:26).

“And We gave them hearing and sight and afʾidah; but their hearing and sight and afʾidah availed them nothing when they rejected the proofs of God; and there surrounded (ḥāqa) them that whereat they mocked.” (Q 46:26)

Thus, the fuʾād represents the inner perceptual awareness that can be stabilized, emptied, or blocked depending on input and calibration. In the text, the fuʾād is never:

Located anatomically

Associated with belief or disbelief

Sealed or hardened by God

Described as diseased

Used as the seat of moral judgment

This is precisely where the Qurʾān distinguishes the fuʾād from the qalb.

Qalb as the Center of Moral Reasoning and Commitment
The word qalb is mentioned 132 times in the Qurʾān. It consistently appears as the faculty of:

reasoning and understanding

belief and hypocrisy

moral turning and corruption

sealing, hardening, and disease

For example:

“In their hearts is a disease…” (Q 2:10)

“God has sealed their hearts…” (Q 2:7)

“Have they not traveled the land so that they may have hearts by which they reason…” (Q 22:46)

In these verses, the operative word for heart is qalb. Unlike fuʾād, qalb is the site of moral judgment and existential response to truth. It is where humans accept, reject, distort, or submit to what is right rather than what is merely beneficial.

The Qurʾān never attributes these moral outcomes to fuʾād.

The Mother of Moses: A Textual Demonstration of the Distinction
The Qurʾān demonstrates its self-referential nature by making the functional separation explicit for these two terms in the account of Moses’ mother:

“The fuʾād of Moses’ mother became empty — had We not bound fast her qalb so she would be among the believers.” (Q 28:10)

Here two inner faculties operate simultaneously within the context of a single crisis event related to letting go of Moses—an act that emotionally consumed a mother in exchange for his future safety and well-being:

fuʾād (emotional reasoning)— overwhelmed by fear and emotional shock

qalb (moral reasoning)— stabilized by God to preserve trust and moral resolve

The Qurʾān’s deliberate choice of fuʾād here conveys the sudden, high-temperature collapse of the innermost, emotionally charged center of her being. Her perceptive consciousness was entirely flooded by the acute crisis, rendering it temporarily empty (fārighā). Yet, while her feelings were in complete chaos, her underlying moral center (qalb) remained anchored because God intervened to bind it fast (rabaṭa) in its orientation.

The juxtaposition of these two words in this verse alone confirms that afʾidah and qalb are non-interchangeable.

Q 22:46 and Q 7:179 — Anatomy Deliberately Linked to Function
This verse provides a different vantage point from which the Qurʾān grounds moral faculties in embodied organs:

“Have they not traveled the land so that they may have hearts (qulub) by which they reason, and ears by which they hear? For it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts (qulub) within the chests (sudur).” (Q 22:46)

Here the text does not speak of abstract faculties, but of:

ears that hear

eyes that see

hearts that reason

This is anatomy directly connected to function. The qalb (heart) is also explicitly and anatomically located within the ṣadr (chest/breast). Crucially, nowhere in the Qurʾān is the ṣadr ever paired with the fuʾād to indicate that the latter possesses an anatomical or bodily home. Again, we note the qalb is also the seat of understanding (yafqahūna):

And We have certainly created for Hell many of the jinn and mankind. They have hearts (qulub) with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. Those are like livestock; rather, they are more astray. It is they who are the heedless. (Q 7:179)

These verses does not say humans reason and understand with afʾidah, nor with the brain. It intentionally locates moral reasoning in the heart — the embodied center of human responsibility.

The case for equating Q 53:11 with Q 22:46—where it could be argued that both fuʾād and qalb are synonyms because both possess a faculty of “sight”—entirely misses the point. One represents an acute faculty registering the immediate reality of what is observed, while the other describes a structural moral failure and a permanent state condition.

In Q 22:46; 7:179:

The sensory eyes see the external evidence flawlessly (the inductive input is fine).

The fuʾād registers the reality of what is being observed.

Because the qalb has undergone a chronic, long-term moral decay (it is diseased, hardened, or sealed due to repeated choices), it has gone blind to the extent that it can no longer see moral truth. This is a structural state condition.

In Q 53:11:

The fuʾād is simply grasping and verifying the acute sensory perception it acquired through its faculties.

It is not describing a chronic, structural state condition that the Qurʾān reserves exclusively for the qalb in Q 22:46; 7:179. Meanwhile, fuʾād never receives anatomical anchoring anywhere in the Qurʾān. It remains a functional perceptive faculty rather than a bodily organ.

Why the Qurʾān Locates Moral Reasoning in the Heart
The Qurʾān’s concept of reasoning (ʿaql) is not mere technical cognition. It consistently refers to:

recognizing truth

restraining from falsehood

responding ethically

committing to guidance

This is purposeful moral-existential reasoning, not merely mathematical or mechanical problem-solving. A person may possess extraordinary computational ability and still act unjustly, and the Qurʾān does not recognize that as true ʿaql. The Qurʾān does not equate intelligence with guidance. Q 22:46 is not about functional IQ that measures an individual on the basis of intellect. Moral intellect and the ability to discern belong to the qalb.

This explains why:

hearts are blind to recognition of truth, not the eyes

hearts are diseased, not afʾidah

hearts contain belief and hypocrisy

Perception remains intact; moral commitment is what fails. The text is simply not interested in a utilitarian IQ operating in isolation from the heart. While value-neutral, instrumental reasoning may exist, the Qur’an does not address it as a separate category. The emphasis is on establishing moral accountability under the Qur’an’s teleological framework—man is under examination and judged by the moral outcomes of his actions.

The Qurʾān consistently assigns moral reasoning, accountability, and evaluative states to qalb, and never to fuʾād; therefore, within the closed Qurʾānic semantic system, qalb is the designated locus of moral reasoning. If the qalb is misaligned and not truth-tracking, the resulting reasoning does not qualify as morally calibrated at all.

Qurʾānic Calibration of Fuʾād and the Qalb
The Qurʾān uses various action verbs for fuʾād and qalb as calibration mechanisms. Consider the following verse:

“…that by which We make firm (nuthabbitu) your fuʾād…” (Q 11:120)

Here ثبّت (thabbata) means to make stable, steady, anchored. Applied to fuʾād, this means inner perceptual awareness is stabilized / anchored under pressure.

“nuthabbitu bihi fuʾādak” → We make firm / stabilize / anchor your fuʾād

thabāt (ثبات) = stability under pressure, not just “strength”

While fuʾād is described by thabāt as a stabilization mechanism, qalb has multi-mode regulation.

Qalb can be made content/given assurance (Q 8:10 li-taṭmaʾinna bihi qulūbukum)

Qalb can be made firm/stabilized (Q 8:11; 25:32 thabāt)

Qalb is the locus of fear and the place for casting terror (Q 8:12; 3:151; 59:2 ruʿ’ba )

Qalb can be restrained/bound (Q 28:10 rabat)

Q 8:10-12 describe how intervention can occur in the qalb during a high pressure battle environment. Reassurance (ṭumaʾnīnah) and firming (thabāt) in the hearts of believers and terror (ruʿ’ba) in the hearts of disbelievers as a destabilizing force, confirm that the qalb is the site of divine intervention under pressure. There is no mention of fuʾād in this scene because the passage is not about perceiving reality. It is about responding to it.

While afʾidah / fuʾād is the site of perception, qalb is the emotional and moral orientation faculty that is the site of selection on how we act on that sensory perception. Reflection (tafakkur) completes the reasoning triad.

Perception awareness needs anchoring. Orientation needs governance. Q 91:7–10 explains the maintenance and degradation mechanics of the internal calibration system.

“By the self (nafs) and He who proportioned it, and inspired it with its deviation and its restraint. Successful is the one who purifies it, and failed is the one who buries it.” (Q 91:7–10)

The nafs is given capacity to recognize deviation and restraint, and its success depends on preserving that signal rather than “burying” it.

When the nafs repeatedly chooses misalignment by following its desires (ahwāʾ) according to Q 30:29, the condition of the qalb (heart) is shaped by this deviation. Persistent avoidance of rightful conduct leads to hardening of the qalb, which is eventually sealed, manifesting as a hardened moral identity.

“Then your hearts became hardened after that, like stones or even harder.” (Q 2:74)

The key insight then is this:

Action → habit → disposition → identity

Thus, the qalb is where moral calibration settles, disobedience becomes identity through repetition, whereas alignment with truth and rightness of action leads to falāḥ — true success. The diagram below shows the complete Qurʾānic architecture of the human moral reasoning system.

Qur’anic Moral Anthropology
The Qur’an corroborates the three human reasoning modes: Deduction, Induction and Abduction. None can operate in isolation.

Deduction (Coherence): Tests the internal fit of ideas within revelation’s logical rule-set and axioms.

Induction (Correspondence): Gathers and processes empirical experience from lived reality.

Abduction (Calibration): Correctly infers the best-fitting explanation based on the moral orientation of the observer.2

All three have to work together to produce intelligence at the nexus of the epistemic-psychological-moral triad.

Without proper qalb calibration, all three can be misapplied. Calibration governs our inner logic and what counts as valid interpretation of reality.

Tafakkur-Tadabbur (coherence) → “what rule set or axiom determines logically sound action?”

Fuʾād (correspondence) → “through inner perceptual awareness, we experience what is happening?”

Qalb (calibration) → “based on its moral state, how do we orient toward it?”

Rather than offering physiological claims, the Qurʾān presents a moral anthropology in which perception remains available to all, while the heart becomes the arena of truth, corruption, and accountability. Reflection (tafakkur) prepares us to take the logically sound action based on sensory perception and orientation.

Conclusion
A disciplined pan-textual reading reveals a precise Qurʾānic system:

fuʾād — the inner perceptive awareness faculty integrating sensory experience, which can be argued to be the seat of human emotional reasoning.

Qalb — the embodied moral-rational center responsible for reasoning, understanding, judgment and commitment.

fuʾād receives reality and registers acute emotional shock. Qalb provides the essential anchoring mechanism and, depending on its calibration, decides how to respond to that reality.

fuʾād is stabilized (thabāt), while the qalb is bound (rabaṭ), stabilized (thabāt), or reassured (ṭumaʾnīnah) as the situation demands. The Qurʾān never collapses the two. No ancient text — biblical, Greek, or philosophical — maintains this level of:

functional separation

consistent terminology

pan-textual precision

Yet the Qurʾān applies it flawlessly across contexts. In Q 22:46, it intentionally grounds moral reasoning in the heart—not as neuroanatomy, but as the locus of human responsibility and conscience. The Qurʾān functionally locates understanding in the qalb as well and not in the mind (Q 7:179). This aligns with universal human experience and preserves the Qurʾān’s internal coherence without ever resorting to scientific harmonization.
