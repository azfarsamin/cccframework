Introduction
Ever since the compilation of Hadith began, there has always been a minority of Muslims—dismissed as fringe by the mainstream—who questioned, and often outright rejected, its canonical validity. They argued against its use in matters of faith, but their voices were systematically marginalized by dominant institutional authorities. While motive is hard to prove, it is well known that religion can be used as a tool to control and manipulate masses — especially those who seek comfort in social consensus and institutional authority. This minority consistently held that true guidance, untainted by outside impurities, is contained solely in the Qur’an. At best, Hadith could be treated as a supplementary historical record offering glimpses of the Prophet Muhammad’s era and the early Islamic community. Yet even this limited utility is further challenged under scrutiny: a careful, objective study of the extraneous literature so readily embraced by traditionalists shows that its reliability as history is itself highly questionable.
Proponents of Hadith often invoke verses from the Qur’an to legitimize the authority of this extra-Quranic literature—but in a manner that is far from convincing. Most arguments in favor of the alleged sayings of the Prophet rely on taking Qur’anic verses out of context or failing to conduct a pan-textual analysis that considers the meaning of terms across different Sūrahs. The topic of Hadith and Sunnah as a source of distortion in what has evolved into the religion of Islam has been covered earlier by other authors. 
In this chapter, I will limit myself to providing evidence from the Qur’an itself. I will show that there is no support for this widely misinterpreted view in favor of Hadith, and the arguments advanced by its apologists to justify a parallel body of work do not cohere on a pan-textual basis. 
The Qur’an’s Usage of the Word Hadith
The Qur’an itself clarifies the meaning of the word hadith through its usage. Derived from the trilateral root ḥ-d-th (ح د ث), it appears 36 times in various forms:
•	As a verb in Form II (تُحَدِّثُ - tuḥaddith) — to narrate, to relate (Q 2:76; 93:11; 99:4)
•	As a verb in Form IV (يُحْدِثُ - yuḥdith) — to bring about, to cause to occur (Q 18:70; 20:113; 65:1)
•	As a noun (حَدِيث - ḥadīth) — speech, statement, discourse (Q 4:42; 4:78; 4:87; 4:140; 6:68; 7:185; 12:6; 12:21; 12:101; 12:111; 18:6; 20:9; 23:44; 31:6; 33:53; 34:19; 39:23; 45:6; 51:24; 52:34; 53:59; 56:81; 66:3; 68:44; 77:50; 79:15; 85:17; 88:1)
•	As a passive participle in Form IV (مُحْدَث - muḥdath) — new, recent (Q 21:2; 26:5)
Taken together, these occurrences make the meaning of Hadith in the Qur’an unambiguous: it refers broadly to speech, narration, discourse, or something new. Crucially, it is never once used to mean “sayings of the Prophet” in the way later Islamic tradition defined the term.
The Qur’an: The Best Hadith
God repeatedly emphasizes in the Qur’an that His Book is the only Hadith worthy of belief. In Sūrah Al-Mursalat, He asks rhetorically:
“Then in which hadith after it (the Qur’an) will they believe?” (Q 77:50)
This challenge is echoed elsewhere:
“In which hadith after this will they believe?” (Q 7:185)
“These are the signs of Allah which We recite to you in truth. So in what hadith after Allah and His signs will they believe?” (Q 45:6)
At times, it appears that the Qur’an’s use of this term anticipates its later misuse by Muslims.
Most strikingly, Allah Himself declares the Qur’an to be the best Hadith:
“God has revealed the most beautiful hadith, a Book consistent, repeating...” (Q 39:23)
The Qur’an even challenges disbelievers to produce a Hadith like it (Q 52:34; 53:59). And in Sūrah Al-Waqi‘ah, it rebukes those who scorn it:
“Is it this hadith (the Qur’an) that you treat with disdain (mudahhinūn)? And have you made your livelihood by denying it?” (Q 56:81–82)
The term mudahhinūn (مُدَهَّنُونَ), derived from the root d-h-n (دهن), suggests concealing opposition in smooth words, evading the truth, or disguising hypocrisy in an appealing manner. 
The Messenger of God strictly adhered to revelation alone. In fact, following anything other than what Allah revealed is not merely discouraged—it is explicitly condemned:
“And among the people are those who purchase the amusement of Hadith (lahw al-hadith) to lead others astray from the path of Allah without knowledge and to make a mockery of it. For them is a humiliating punishment.” (Q 31:6)
Traditional translators have rendered lahw al-hadith as “frivolous tales,” while many South Asian Hadith scholars have explained it as ghanaa (“singing.”) But if Allah had meant “singing,” why would He not have said so directly? This interpretation is both forced and misleading.
The verse itself highlights two defining traits of lahw al-hadith: 
•	it diverts people from Allah’s path, and 
•	it is not based on knowledge. 
In light of how the Qur’an uses the word Hadith elsewhere, interpreting it merely as “singing,” “frivolous tales” or “amusement of speech” strips the verse of its true force. In reality, lahw al-hadith refers to stories and traditions that mislead from the truth. Those who “purchase” or embrace such Hadith are warned of severe punishment in the Hereafter.
These verses carry a sobering warning for religions where the responsibility of spreading God’s message has been professionalized, and secondary texts have been elevated over divine revelation. The Qur’an itself cautions that later generations often distort divine revelation, obscure its meaning, and sell it cheaply for worldly gain (Q 2:75; 2:79; 2:174; 3:199; 4:46; 5:13; 5:41; 5:44; 9:9).
Sunnah of God or Sunnah of the Prophet?
In traditional Hadith scholarship, Sunnah refers to all the sayings, actions, and tacit approvals of the Prophet — that is, anything he said, did, or remained silent about. This definition extends not only to his religious rulings but also to his personal habits, such as eating, drinking, or dressing. According to this view, the Sunnah is preserved and transmitted primarily through Hadith literature.
Some modern scholars propose a different understanding: they argue that Sunnah represents the practical dimension of Islam established by the Prophet and passed down to the community through consensus and continuous transmission (tawatur). Examples include prayer, fasting, Hajj, and zakat. In this perspective, Sunnah is less about individual narrations and more about a living, communal practice maintained across generations.
Our purpose here is not to delve into the fine details of these scholarly opinions, but rather to highlight a crucial point: the meanings of Sunnah as understood in Islamic discourse today find no basis in the Qur’an itself.
The word Sunnah appears sixteen times in eleven distinct verses in the Qur’an  (Q 3:137; 4:26; 8:38; 15:13; 17:77; 18:55; 33:38; 33:62; 35:43; 40:85; 48:23). In every case, it refers to the Sunnah (Practice) of Allah —that is, His unchanging laws governing the fate of nations and the consequences of human behavior.
“The Sunnah of Allah applies to those who came before, and you will find no change in the Sunnah of Allah.” (Q 33:62)
The Qur’an speaks only of the Sunnah of Allah. Nowhere does it refer to a Sunnah of the Prophet.
The burden of proof rests on traditionalists to demonstrate where the primary text uses the terms Hadith and Sunnah to refer to an extra-scriptural corpus of prophetic sayings and actions.
Does what the Messenger gives or forbids mean Hadith?
Let’s start with the low-hanging fruit before moving on to arguments that require deeper analysis. The following fragment of a verse is often cited to suggest that the Qur’an endorses Hadith, provides details on certain matters, or instructs Muslims to obey the Prophet even after his passing:
“…And what the Messenger gives you: take it, and from what he forbids you: refrain…” (Q 59:7)
But what is the Messenger giving that must be taken and refrained from when forbidden? The reality is that scholars of Hadith quote part of a verse out of context to legitimize a secondary literature. When we look at the full verse and the surrounding text, the reality is quite different. 
Now, consider the verse block Q 59:5-7:
What (mā) you cut down of palm-trees (Q 59:5)
And 	What (mā) God restored to His messenger from them (palm trees) (Q 59:6)
What (mā) God restored to His messenger from the people of the cities
belongs to God and His messenger, 
and to relatives, and the fatherless, 
and the needy, and the wayfarer[...]
that it be not a distribution by turns between the rich among you.
And what (mā) the Messenger gives you: take it, 
And  what (mā) he forbids you: refrain (Q:59:7)
Consider the sequence Q 59:5-7 in which the mā “what” (a relative pronoun) serves as the direct object and ties every sentence together. It is pointing back to the spoils being apportioned and disseminated among the less fortunate to prevent wealth from being circulated purely among the wealthy. The grammar structure is unambiguous and precise. The Prophet’s authority in Q 59:7 was administrative and executive oversight to implement God’s commands. Using the clause to smuggle in an independent, extra-Quranic religious work and grant it prophetic authority is a non sequitur: the conclusion simply doesn’t follow from the premise. 
When read in full, the verse block is semantically and grammatically bounded, demonstrating the tamper-resistant feature of the Qur’an.
Does al-Hikmah in the Qur’an Mean Hadith?
The Qur’anic term al-Hikmah is often misrepresented as synonymous with Hadith literature. While it is usually translated as “wisdom,” some early scholars, such as Imam Shafi‘i (d. 204 AH), argued that this term refers to the Prophet’s sayings, which were later compiled as Hadith. Apologists further anchor this claim in the following Hadith:
Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4604, attributed to al-Miqdām ibn Maʿdīkarib. Muhammad is reported to have said:
“I have been given the Qur’an and something like it along with it…”
Not only is the Hadith vague and does not explicitly mention al-Hikmah, more problematic is that using Hadith to validate itself is circular reasoning. The reality is that al-Hikmah, as employed by the traditionalists, is a reified term  doing a lot of epistemic work that needs to be unpacked. We need to let the Qur’an define its own terms, so let’s look at the pan-textual evidence. Consider the following verse often cited in support of this argument:
“God indeed showed favour to the believers when He raised up among them a messenger among themselves, reciting to them His proofs, and increasing them in purity, and teaching them the Book and Wisdom — though they were before in manifest error.” (Q 3:164)
We note that wisdom is not limited to being given to Prophet Muhammad:
•	Q 2:251 — David is given wisdom
•	Q 2:269 — God gives wisdom to whom He wills
•	Q 3:48; 5:110 — Jesus is taught wisdom
•	Q 3:81; 6:89 — Prophets are given wisdom
•	Q 4:54 — The house of Abraham is given wisdom
•	Q 31:12 — Luqman is given wisdom
The list above shows Q 3:164 cannot be taken in isolation to map al-Hikmah to Hadith — the pan-textual evidence is to the contrary. Many prophets and messengers including the family of Abraham were endowed with wisdom:
If they envy men what God gave them of His bounty: then We had given the house of Abraham the Writ and wisdom; and We gave them great dominion. (Q 4:54)
It is implausible that this al-Hikmah would mean authorizing literature compiled centuries later and attributing it to the Prophet.
In Q 2:231, al-Hikmah is explicitly mentioned alongside al-Kitab (wa mā anzala 'alaykum min al-kitābi wa-l-ḥikmah) and it is stated clearly that it is sent down along with al-Kitab (writ/book). 
“…and remember the favour of God upon you, and what He has sent down to you of the Book and wisdom whereby He admonishes you…” (Q 2:231)
What is this admonition? In Sūrah Al-Isrā’ (Q 17:22–39), after laying down a series of moral, social, and spiritual commands, the Qur’an explicitly defines its own wisdom:
“That is from what thy Lord has revealed to thee of wisdom (al-Hikmah). And make thou not with God another god lest thou be cast into Gehenna, blameworthy and banished.” (Q 17:39)
Here, the Qur’an leaves no room for ambiguity: al-Hikmah refers to the core moral truths, understanding, and ethical principles revealed within the text itself. In the same way, Luqman imparts wisdom to his son (Q 31:12-19). 
We also find elsewhere that al-Hikmah  can be granted to anyone to whom God wills:
He gives wisdom to whom He wills; and to whom wisdom is given, he has been given much good; but only those of insight take heed. (Q 2:269)
This verse alone exposes the fragility of the traditionalist’s position, demonstrating that true wisdom is a gift from God rather than a label for a secondary canon ascribed to an individual who doesn’t even know it exists. 
But the Qur’an’s tamper-proofing for this term doesn’t end here. It creates a logical problem for the apologist when his own contradictory claims collide with one another.
Al-Hikmah: An Epistemic Fork for the Hadith Apologist
Classical theologians have categorized Hadith as Waḥy Ghayr Matluww (“unrecited revelation”) to distinguish it from the Qur'an. However, this theological maneuver collapses when confronted with Sūrah Al-Aḥzāb:
“And remember what is recited (yutla) within your houses of the verses (ayahs) of God and of wisdom (al-Hikmah); God is subtle and aware.” (Q 33:34)
Q 33:34 explicitly states that al-Hikmah is recited (yutlā). Since Hadith literature is never recited in prayers or ritual, this creates a  fatal fork for the apologist:
EITHER Hadith is al-Hikmah, which directly contradicts Q 33:34 (since Hadith is not recited scripture)
OR Hadith is "unrecited revelation," which invalidates the claim that al-Hikmah in Q 33:34 refers to Hadith.
Pan-textual analysis refutes the claim that al-Hikmah in the Qur’an refers to Hadith. It is the divine revelation itself—pure, self-contained, and independent of any later human compilation. There is no concept of “unrecited revelation” that can be gleaned from the primary text. Revelation itself is sufficient and recited (Q 29:51). This link was created later by classical exegetes to legitimize exogenous literature, repeating the same mistakes committed by earlier Abrahamic traditions. The Qur’an is very critical of those who twist God’s words from their places (Q 5:13; 5:41).
Does the term Us’watan Hasanatun Endorse Hadith?
Another verse often cited by Muslim scholars to justify the Hadith literature reads:
“Indeed, in the Messenger of God a good example (us’watan hasanatun) has been set for you—for he who seeks God and the Last Day and remembers God much.” (Q 33:21)
The term us’watan hasanatun is usually translated as “good example” or “role model.” Scholars present this as evidence that Muslims must follow Hadith and Sunnah, but the surrounding context tells a different story. The verse pertain to a specific event of battle during the Prophet’s life. God is highlighting the Prophet’s moral and spiritual qualities:
•	He seeks God and the Last Day.
•	He remembers God often.
A pan-textual analysis reveals that this term appears three times in the Qur’an. In the second instance, God directs believers to follow the example of Abraham (Q 60:4):
“A good example (us’watan hasanatun) has been set by Abraham and those with him, when they declared to their people: ‘We are innocent of you and what you serve besides God…” (Q 60:4)
Here Abraham is setting a good example by anchoring himself to serving God alone and disavowing idol worship. The final instance of us’watan hasanatun is provided in Q 60:6 where the believers at the time of Abraham are themselves setting a good example: they are true monotheists and know in their hearts that there will be a Day of Reckoning when all accounts will be settled.
“Certainly, a good example (us’watan hasanatun) has been set by them for those who seek God and the Last Day. And whoever turns away, God is the Rich, the Praiseworthy.” (Q 60:6)
Clearly, in all three cases, us’watan hasanatun refers to moral and spiritual conduct, not to a compilation of sayings to be codified and followed by later generations. Extending the verse to mean emulating the Prophet’s clothing, eating habits, or other mundane actions is a non sequitur — the conclusion does not follow from the Qur’an’s own verdict.
This creates a fatal fork for the traditionalist position:
•	EITHER us’watan ḥasanatan refers to a compiled body of Hadith and Sunnah, which directly contradicts Q 60:4 and Q 60:6 since no such extra-scriptural record attributed to Abraham or his followers exists today;
•	OR us’watan ḥasanatan refers to exemplary moral and spiritual conduct, which invalidates the claim that Q 33:21 serves as a mandate for Hadith literature.
Circumstantial evidence so far is strong to suggest motive: Islamic exegetes took verses out of context to support ideas the Qurʾān itself never authorizes. Followers then accept these explanations trusting the authority they come from, without realizing that the reasoning does not actually come from the Qurʾān.
The Prophet Followed Only Wahi (Revelation)
As I explained in Chapter 5, the Qur’an itself shows that when people around the Prophet had questions, God answered them directly by sending down revelation (wahi). In all these instances, the recurring structure is: “They ask you… Say…” (yasʾalūnaka / yastaftūnaka… qul…), showing that clarification always came from God, not Muhammad’s personal opinion.
While Muhammad was indeed a teacher to the believers, his role was strictly limited to following what was revealed to him (Q 6:50; 7:203; 10:15; 10:109; 11:12; 18:27; 20:13; and 46:9). He was prohibited from fabricating anything on his own (Q 66:1; 69:44–47). Yet in Hadith literature, we find numerous sayings attributed to him that not only lack Qur’anic confirmation but often contradict its clear guidance.
Just as God commanded earlier prophets and their communities to follow divine scriptures, the final Prophet and his followers were given the same command:
“Say: I do not tell you that I possess Allah’s treasures, nor do I know the unseen, nor do I say that I am an angel. I only follow what is revealed to me. Say: Are the blind and the seeing equal? Do you not reflect?” (Q 6:50)
The declaration “I only follow what is revealed to me” (إِنْ أَتَّبِعُ إِلَّا مَا يُوحَىٰٓ إِلَيَّ) appears repeatedly in the Qur’an (Q 7:203; 10:15; 46:9). This consistency, from beginning to end, makes it clear that there is no divine directive to believe in Hadith or Sunnah alongside God’s Book. 
Prophet’s Authority Over Lawfulness and Unlawfulness
The Hadith literature has many alleged sayings attributed to the Prophet where he is seen to exercise his authority to make things lawful or unlawful. But here is the foundational question: Who has the authority to declare what is lawful and unlawful? Our reference point is, of course, the Qur’an. Can it be anyone other than God?
In Q 66:1, we see Prophet Muhammad himself being corrected by God for prohibiting something merely to please his wives:
“O Prophet, why do you forbid (tuḥarrimu) what God has made lawful (aḥalla) for you, seeking to please your wives? God is Forgiving, Merciful. God has ordained for you the absolution of your oaths. And God is your Protector, and He is the Knowing, the Wise.” (Q 66:1–2)
The message here is unambiguous: the authority to declare something unlawful belongs to God alone. Not even His final messenger was permitted to impose such restrictions. The Prophet is reminded to dissolve his oath and free himself from an unnecessary self-imposed burden.
Notably, the Qur’an does not elaborate on the background of this event, which suggests that the specific details are unimportant. What matters is the principle: no one—Prophet or otherwise—has the right to forbid what God has allowed.
This raises a serious issue when we turn to the Hadith literature, where many reports attribute to the Prophet sayings that appear to prohibit things. Since the Qur’an emphasizes that the Prophet followed only what was revealed to him (e.g., Q 10:15, 46:9), it is difficult to reconcile such reports with scripture. The most reasonable conclusion is that these attributions do not originate with him but were later inventions in his name.
The Seriousness of Fabricating Law
The gravity of declaring something ḥalāl or ḥarām without God’s authority is underscored in the following verses:
“And do not say, concerning the falsehood your tongues describe: ‘This is lawful (ḥalāl), and this is forbidden (ḥarām),’ inventing lies against God. Indeed, those who invent lies against God will never succeed. A brief enjoyment, then they will face a painful punishment.” (Q 16:116–117)
These verses leave no room for doubt: attributing false rulings to God is a form of blasphemy that leads to certain punishment. If such a warning applies to ordinary people, it follows all the more that God’s final messenger—who was fully aware of the seriousness of fabricating in God’s name—would never engage in declaring unlawful what God had permitted. This reinforces the conclusion that reports which attribute such prohibitions to him cannot be genuine, but must have been later fabrications. This is confirmed unequivocally in the Qur’an:
“[This is] a revelation from the Lord of all creation. And if he [the Prophet] had fabricated any statements and attributed them to Us, We would have seized him by the right hand, then cut his aorta, and none of you could have shielded him.” (Q 69:43–47)
The severity of these words leaves no doubt: fabricating revelations in God’s name is unthinkable, even for the Prophet himself. If he had done so, God makes clear that He would have ended his mission immediately.
In light of this, it is difficult to accept that the Prophet would later be portrayed as declaring things unlawful that God had not forbidden—especially in collections compiled two to three centuries after his death, which he neither saw nor authorized. Such attributions deserve critical re-examination by those who seek to practice faith on the basis of God’s own revelation.
Only God Determines Lawfulness
Another verse highlights the principle that humans cannot legislate in God’s stead:
“Say: ‘Have you considered what God has provided for you, yet you have made some of it unlawful (ḥarām) and some lawful (ḥalāl)?’ Say: ‘Did God give you permission, or do you invent lies against God?’” (Q 10:59)
God firmly rebukes those who take it upon themselves to issue religious rulings without His authority. The Qur’an makes it clear that only God determines what is lawful and unlawful, and these rulings are already specified in His revelation.
Muhammad could not:
•	invent religious sayings,
•	issue new laws,
•	make things lawful or unlawful,
•	legislate independently of revelation,
•	or add anything to the religion.
God further provides the example of the Children of Israel, who committed a similar transgression for which they were punished as a lesson for future generations (Q 3:93; 4:160; 6:146).
Notably, some Hadith later included similar prohibitions, such as forbidding animals with claws and birds with talons. 
Sahih Muslim Book 34. Hadith 4992:
It was narrated from Abu Hurairah that the Prophet (s.a.w) said: "Every wild animal that has fangs, eating it is Haram (forbidden)." 
While these restrictions were originally specific to the Children of Israel, it appears that some Muslim compilers incorporated elements of Jewish dietary law into the Hadith corpus, which later acquired canonical status.
In a similar manner, the Qur’an notes that pagan Arabs also engaged in prohibitions that made little common sense and falsely attributed them to God (Q 6:138–139; 6:143–144).
The discussion of what is ḥalāl and ḥarām extends beyond food and drink to other aspects of life. Numerous Hadith, for example, prohibit Muslims from wearing gold rings, silk clothing, or garments dyed with saffron, and even disallow keeping dogs as pets. The point here is that, regardless of personal preference or cultural norms, the Qur’an establishes that no one—not even the Prophet —has the authority to forbid what God has made permissible, contrary to the claims of Hadith apologists.
Say, "Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah which He has produced for His servants and the good [lawful] things of provision?" Say, "They are for those who believe during the worldly life [but] exclusively for them on the Day of Resurrection." Thus do We detail the verses for a people who know. (Q 7:32) 
This verse makes clear that:
•	God provides the details, not humanly written texts, and
•	There are no inherent restrictions on lawful luxury items. 
As long as a person observes God’s guidance and understands that this life determines judgment in the Hereafter, there is no reason they cannot enjoy the blessings God has provided. The final part of the verse further reinforces a point highlighted earlier: the Qur’an provides all necessary guidance to follow God alone, without reliance on supplementary books or man-made religious traditions that the Prophet never authorized.
Here is the legal dilemma for those who rely on Hadith as an authoritative source of what is lawful (ḥalāl) and unlawful (ḥarām):
•	EITHER Prohibition (taḥrīm) belongs strictly to God alone, as confirmed by God’s correction of the Prophet in Q 66:1 and the strict warnings in Q 16:116 and Q 69:43–47;
•	OR The Prophet exercised independent authority to declare permissible things ḥarām, which directly contradicts Q 66:1 and implies a violation of the boundaries set in Q 69:43–47.
It is evident that only God has the authority to declare what is lawful (ḥalāl) and unlawful (ḥarām), and no human being has been granted the power to override the criteria established in the Qur’an.
Hadith “Details” the Qur’an
Hadith is commonly presented as necessary to “explain” the Qur’an or provide “details” to fill in the gaps. But its actual doctrinal function extends far beyond explanation: it creates independent laws, punishments, articles of faith, and even claims of revelation no longer present in the Qur’anic text.

A few examples taken from the vast sectarian corpora, shown in Table 4.5 expose the problem. These are not explanations of Qur’anic injunctions. They are  independent propositions that do not originate from the primary text. Stoning does not explain one hundred lashes for zina; a missing stoning verse does not explain the preserved Qur’an; apostasy execution does not explain a Qur’anic apostasy penalty because no such penalty exists; and Dajjāl or Mahdi do not elaborate Qur’anic eschatological figures because the Qur’an never mentions either of them.
While the secondary corpus is framed as merely commentary on revelation, it functions as additional source of revelation-like legislation and theology. 
The Hadith self-validation is exposed through the following circularity:
Qur’an has “missing details” → Hadith is required to explain the Qur’an → Hadith introduces new doctrines and legal rulings → Those doctrines are declared authoritative because they come from “Prophetic tradition” → Their absence from the Qur’an is cited as proof that the Qur’an has “missing details”
The “explanation” defense therefore creates a dilemma:
•	EITHER identify the Qur’anic propositions these Hadith are supposedly explaining and demonstrate that they function as explanations of the primary text;
•	OR concede that these Hadith do not merely explain the Qur’an, but introduce independent doctrines, laws, punishments and articles of faith absent from it.

Obey the Messenger
Another collocation frequently misused by religious scholars to insist on the canonical status of Hadith is the command:
“And obey God and the Messenger, that you might obtain mercy.” (Q 3:132)
This construction occurs 20 times in the Qur’an (Q 3:32; 3:132; 4:13; 4:59; 4:69; 5:92; 8:1; 8:20; 8:46; 9:71; 24:52; 24:54; 33:33; 33:66; 33:71; 47:33; 48:17; 49:14; 58:13; 64:12) and requires deeper analysis.
The traditional mainstream argument essentially is that:
•	“Obey God” means following the Qur’an and
•	“Obey the Messenger” means following Hadith literature and the Sunnah of the Prophet. 
As established earlier in Chapter 4, Hadith:
•	Suffers from sectarian fragmentation
•	Elevates alleged sayings of the Prophet to a divine status
•	Requires an unfalsifiable methodology of human authentication within a closed system to hold it together
•	Consists of multiple textual genealogies that were only later standardized
•	Introduces new doctrines alien to the primary text
•	Requires its followers to treat it as a theologically binding corpus
So the construction “Obey the messenger” in isolation — if understood as a later Hadith corpus — is acting as a reified term by taking an abstract Qur’anic collocation and assigning it a physical value.  So we will let the Qur’an deliver its own verdict.
Argument from Semantic Equivalence
Consider the following construction:
Say: “Obey God and obey the Messenger.” Then if you turn away, then upon him (fa-innama ‘alayhi) is what he has been given to bear, and upon you is what you have been given to bear (Q 24:54)
Notice the clause following “obey God and the Messenger”: fa-innama ‘alayhi—where ‘alayhi uses a 3rd person masculine singular object pronoun pointing back strictly to the Messenger. It is not a dual object pronoun (‘alayhima) taking “God and the Messenger” as two distinct lawgiving antecedents. This is not a language requirement, but it does tell us that the messenger takes his cue from God. The unified divine authority exercised through the messenger
Argument from Grammar
Traditionalists often attempt to bypass this singular framing, but it is a consistent, pan-textual feature across the Qur’an. A clear parallel appears in Sūrah At-Tawbah:
They swear by God to you in order to please you. But God and His Messenger—it is more fitting that they should please Him (yurduhu), if they are believers. (Q 9:62)
Here again, the object pronoun is the masculine singular hu (“please Him”), not the dual or plural. Non-sectarian translations (such as A.J. Arberry’s) render it faithfully according to the words on the page: “more right is it they should please Him.” Traditionalist translators, however, routinely execute grammatical gymnastics to force their narrative—with Saheeh International rendering it as “please Allah and His Messenger” and Hilali & Khan inserting “please Allah and His Messenger (Muhammad SAW)”.
Even traditionalist commentators like Muhammad Asad (citing Rashid Rida) concede that there is no juxtaposition or dual-authority structure here; the singular pronoun brings out the core truth that a believer’s duty to follow the Prophet is merely the direct outcome of accepting the divine message he delivers.
Yet, this exposes the remarkably tamper-resistant nature of the Qur’an: while sectarian translators can manipulate English renderings to invent parallel authority structures, the immutable Arabic original continuously resists human interpolation, using its own internal syntax to expose these distortions.
Argument from Physical Presence
Imagine living during that time when the Qur'an was being revealed through the Messenger. The only way to hear the revelation was through the Prophet himself. Therefore, when the collocation “obey God and the Messenger” is used, it clearly means obeying what God was revealing through Muhammad. The Qur’an’s grammar confirms this practical reality. 
Obeying the Messenger applied directly to those who received revelation from him in real time. Later generations can only obey the message he delivered, not the person. As with earlier communities, when the messenger passed away, the opportunity for direct obedience ended. 
This fits a broader pattern seen throughout history. Every messenger was instructed to tell his community to obey him, because obedience to the messenger was synonymous with obedience to God. The Qur’an emphasizes that Muhammad was not introducing a new religion, but confirming the same call made by earlier messengers. In Q 26:104–179, we hear the same refrain from Noah, Hud, Salih, Lot, and Shuʿayb:
“I am to you a trustworthy messenger.
So be in prudent fear of God, and obey me.”
Likewise, Isa (Jesus) tells the Children of Israel:
“So be in prudent fear of God, and obey me.” (Q 3:50; 43:63)
And we see Noah repeating the same message in Q 71:3:
“Serve God, and be in prudent fear of Him, and obey me. (Q 71:3)
This was the consistent pattern: obeying the messenger in one’s own time was equivalent to obeying God and the revelation He sent through that messenger. But to claim that later generations were authorized to compose new literature and attach it to the messenger on the basis of this collocation is a conclusion that doesn’t follow from the premises. Yet many Muslims today set aside the Qur’an in favor of the post-Qur’anic compilations of Bukhari and Muslim—texts that distort the Qur’anic call to follow Muhammad by reducing it to obedience to later human authorities.
Argument from Pan-Textual Analysis
The traditionalist claim that “obey the messenger” authorizes a second body of religious literature cannot be evaluated in isolation. It must be reconciled with the Qur’an's repeated statements concerning the messenger's knowledge, authority, obligations, limitations, and relationship to revelation.
The following pan-textual evidence establishes the boundaries within which the command must be understood.

Muhammad repeatedly declares that he follows only what is revealed to him. The Qur’an defines the source of his religious guidance rather than leaving it open to independent pronouncements.

Taken together, these verses establish a consistent chain of religious authority: God reveals, the messenger follows and conveys what is revealed, and believers obey the divine message communicated through him.
The traditionalist interpretation reverses this relationship by transforming a command to obey God's messenger into authorization for a separate body of religious literature that the messenger neither compiled nor authenticated.
The burden of proof is therefore straightforward: Where does the Qur’an authorize the transformation of obedience to a living messenger into perpetual obedience to binding posthumous reports attributed to him?
Repeating the instruction to “obey the messenger” does not answer that question. It merely restates the command as a placeholder whose scope is under examination.

The Logical Fork for "Obey the Messenger":
•	EITHER "Obey the Messenger" is a grammatical and historical command to follow the living Prophet as he delivered and implemented the revelation;
•	OR "Obey the Messenger" is an open-ended mandate for secondary literature, which forces a dual-authority model onto a text that lexically, grammatically and pan-textually contradicts it violating Q 4:82.


Conclusion
In this chapter, I examined the religious status of Hadith and Sunnah, showing through a careful textual analysis of the Qur’an that neither concept, as understood today, has any foundation in Allah’s final revelation. The matter reduces to a simple truth: the Prophet’s message was the Qur’an—nothing more, nothing less.
The Qur’an was revealed to him; he was commanded to recite it, convey it, teach it, and live by it. He recited it, had it written down, reminded others of it, and followed it himself. Allah Himself took responsibility for its preservation. Can the same be said of Hadith?
The Prophet followed nothing except the Qur’an, and as the Messenger, that alone was his entrusted message to the community. For any Muslim who reflects with understanding, the Qur’an by itself is sufficient as complete guidance for a successful life—both in this world and the next.
The majority of the followers have been conditioned since birth to accept the institutionalized narrative without verification, and those who dare challenge these inconsistencies face ridicule, name-calling, and even excommunication.
