Chapter 1

  Evaluating Texts Methodologically


The Paradox of Disagreement
     I have always been a strong proponent of the view that humans should engage in
effective reasoning rather than assumption, hearsay or listening to authority blindly to
reach correct conclusions. Procedurally, the assumption is that we form hypotheses,
collect facts or data through observation, apply deductive reasoning — the rules and
axioms we have learned throughout our lives — to filter experiential data, and on that
basis determine, using the evidence at our disposal, what the correct version of truth
is.
   But this process, which seems so rational, always bothered me. If this is true, why
do people disagree all the time? If the facts are neutral and the same for everyone in the
reality we experience around us, why do humans invariably reach different
conclusions about truth? The search for an answer to this seemingly underappreciated
question, which occupied my mind for years, culminated in my first book, Reason,
Revelation, and the Architecture of Truth: A Framework for Evaluating Worldviews.
    This work led me to the key breakthrough insight that:
    Participants argue at the level of conclusions, while their real disagreement exists
at the level of foundations.
    Disagreement occurs as humans form assumptions upstream that neither cohere
internally nor fit experience and observation when treated as a whole. They use their
internal rules, reconciling them with experiential data, to reach conclusions that are at
odds with other humans who engage in a similar exercise with a different set of
assumptions, axioms and lived experiences.
    This motivated a need to create:
    a shared set of standard criteria against which all worldviews could be evaluated.

After much reflection, I figured that the worldview that most reliably guides
human thought must be internally coherent, correspond closely to reality and must
provide a method for distinguishing truth from error at the level of first principles.
This book takes the reader through an intellectual journey of finding the religious text
that most reliably satisfies these criteria with the fewest assumptions and lowest

The Three-Legged Stool: Coherence, Correspondence and
Calibration
     I have always been fascinated by solving problems not in isolation, but by
applying systems thinking principles. Most of us have experienced the frustration of
building a jigsaw puzzle—imagine building one without the image on the box lid.
Individual puzzle pieces are meaningless on their own. We cannot see the picture, and
a piece has no utility until it connects to others. The image on the box lid serves as the
ultimate guide. Without it, assembling a complex puzzle is painstakingly slow and
disorganized.
     I proceed from a similar, daunting constraint: constructing a jigsaw puzzle of
reality without knowing what it looks like. Worse yet, we must discover the individual
pieces ourselves using our internal jigsaw assembly engine—the triadic ability to
observe, reason, and evaluate. The only way to know if we have built a correct model
of the world is when those pieces click into a coherent whole.
    This is what I call Coherence: ensuring the model provides a non-contradictory
way of arriving at a correct interpretation without breaking the internal logical
structure. When applied to evaluating scripture, the textual, grammatical, semantic,
narrative and doctrinal analysis should remain logically consistent across the text, so
that the model does not require ad hoc rescue every time it fails to cohere globally.
    This type of thinking relies primarily on what we normally refer to as deductive
reasoning—moving from a general rule to a specific case ensuring all propositions hold
without introducing any logical gaps that cannot be reconciled when the system is
treated as a whole. Any contradiction or a blind spot in our understanding introduces
an explanatory burden and deteriorates model quality.

Coherence alone, however, is not enough. It must correspond with true lived
experience. A completely fictional account such as J. K. Rowling’s Harry Potter can
also be perfectly coherent internally. But the magic wand can only take us so far in the
real world. This is why we need Correspondence—a way to ensure that the text or a
worldview is in alignment with observation and lived experience. This requires
inductive reasoning: a way to test theory by empirically observing the world around
us. To determine whether reality agrees with the rules and axioms of our deductive
model.
   Both coherence and correspondence, however, are still insufficient to determine
truth. A child born and raised in the Wizarding World of Harry Potter at Universal
Studios, and taught the seven-book anthology as past history, can genuinely believe he
is living in Hogwarts Castle among wizards in a magical world. To those trapped
inside, the internal mythos—the complex set of beliefs, values, or narratives that
characterize this fictional world—can seem perfectly coherent. Their sensory input
corresponds with their immediate surroundings. What they lack is an external
reference standard that can detect error and help them recognize that they are living
inside a manufactured ecosystem enclosed by a larger, unyielding reality.
    Thus, in order to detect inner logical inconsistencies and recognize when reality
diverges from theory, we need a third axis to form hypotheses and test them based on a
criterion of truth. This axis enables us to select the best explanation on the basis of the
totality of evidence. This is arguably the least understood, and I call it Calibration.
    This triad completes the CCC (Coherence-Correspondence-Calibration)
Framework.
    Calibration refers to a governing standard that is itself part of reality. Every
human being is architecturally required to exercise judgment in making decisions. No
one is immune. Even a nihilist or a radical skeptic cannot operate in the world without
this axis, because the denial of all meaning is itself a position regardless of whether it is
right or wrong. Calibration acts as the bridge between the internal lens of the observer
(subjective orientation) and the external architecture of reality (objective constraint).

In the framework, the calibration axis is associated with abductive reasoning,
which humans use alongside deduction and induction to formulate hypotheses and
determine which explanation provides the best fit with the fewest assumptions.
   Each reasoning mode is incomplete in isolation:
    •   Pure deduction → closed system (generates no new information)
    •   Pure induction → chaotic noise (accumulates unorganized data)
    •   Pure abduction → unfalsifiable speculation (collapses into hallucination or
        guesswork)
   The triad functions together as an interlocking system to produce intelligence, or
more accurately, to generate inferences about the reality around us.

    This conclusion is structural—like a three-legged stool. Just as removing one leg
would collapse the stool, removing one reasoning mode from the triad would no
longer produce any intelligence.
    This makes the CCC Framework cognitively native to the way human reasoning
works. To summarize:

•   Coherence: maps to deduction and tests internal validity within a
            worldview’s axioms.
       •   Correspondence: maps to induction and tests external fit with observable
               reality.
       •   Calibration: maps to abduction and applies an external constraint
           through abductive reasoning to select the best explanation.
   As we shall see later, the calibration axis is the most important because it not only
governs coherence and correspondence but even shapes our knowing. It can be argued
that what we choose to accept as knowledge is conditioned by how we choose to
calibrate ourselves.
   The question then arises: what criteria must determine a calibration that justifies
what is considered as knowledge and brings us closer to the truth?

Humans Cannot Ground Truth: A Glitch in Reality?
  My journey toward finding a definite answer to this question led me to a
remarkable realization: that nearly 2000 years of philosophical inquiry has long
wrestled with the nature of truth and has concluded that any justification of
knowledge eventually reaches one of three dead ends:
    •   Circular justification—the conclusion feeds back into the premise.
    •   Regresses forever—every justification requires another justification.
    •   Dogmatic stop— declare something ‘self-evident’ without proof because it
         just is.
    These failures form the three horns of Agrippa’s Trilemma (also known as the
Münchhausen Trilemma), articulated in ancient Pyrrhonian skepticism and
preserved in Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism.
   The trilemma is a structural property of the reality we experience. Neither I nor
anyone else can absolutely prove claims, such as whether God exists or where we came
from, from within human reason alone. It is an inescapable feature of the overarching
epistemological architecture. It applies to all human knowledge systems, including
physics and math (e.g., explanation of gravity, Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems). No
worldview is immune to it.

===== Chapter 1, printed page 6, PDF page 18 =====
The trilemma reveals an important insight of epistemic humility:
   Knowledge systems cannot fully ground truth from within a closed human
system.
    In other words, uncertainty is inherently embedded into reality. It cannot be
avoided. Because truth cannot be fully grounded by any human system, the rational
goal is not absolute certainty, but closeness to truth. This is accomplished by
introducing an external calibration point—outside of the worldviews under
examination—by which truth can be approached through comparison—specifically
asking which competing explanations best satisfy the shared constraints imposed by
reality itself. This ties in nicely with the CCC Framework introduced earlier.
   The CCC Framework isn’t about absolute justification to select a worldview. It is
about comparative superiority under shared evaluative constraints.
   The trilemma insight has huge implications: it shows that disagreement is
inevitable because every worldview begins from unprovable first principles. The
solution then is not a collapse into dogmatism, relativism, or radical skepticism but
comparative evaluation. A method utilizing a shared set of criteria to decide which
worldview sustains coherence, correspondence, and calibration consistently to provide
the best explanatory fit. A worldview that cannot adjudicate between competing
explanations and lacks the ability to self-correct cannot guide belief or action.
   The key insight is this:
   Humans cannot ground truth. The best we can do is use comparative evaluation
as a proxy for truth tracking.
   While this helps us understand the constraints of the underlying architecture of
reality that every living man and woman faces to reason and make decisions, the
problem is still not fully resolved. Everyone can agree that arguments must be
coherent and correspond with reality, but it is along the calibration axis where the
bulk of disagreement occurs — when humans frame their arguments from assumed
conclusions that are already baked into the premise.

  We can think of calibration as the internal state of “being” of an individual that

Truth Lies in the Whole, Not the Parts
   The calibration problem is further compounded by reductionism—partial
observations that are locally coherent but cannot explain reality in its totality. A siloed
thinking approach can provide better economic incentives in today’s world for
specialists but can put humans at a disadvantage when developing an integrated
macro view of the world. The blind-men-and-elephant parable shown in Figure 1.3
is a great metaphor for how we think about a complex problem from our own
vantage points. Our local conclusions are individually reasonable but risk collective
incoherence if other important variables are not taken into account.
  A belief system that is incomplete can seem coherent to its adherents within its
own boundaries but cannot reveal the blind spots until as much available information
as humanly possible is taken into account to explain reality. The calibration axis of the
CCC Framework is dynamic: it should provide the precise diagnostic toolkit needed
to step back, recognize the global incoherence and partial correspondence of these
isolated observers, and reveal the actual architecture of the elephant before their
localized interpretations collapse under their own structural contradictions.

Distorted Representations of Reality
   As I argued earlier, facts do not interpret themselves. The way our beliefs are
calibrated determines how we interpret facts. And when our calibration is wrong, we
can mistake distorted representations of reality for reality itself. As we saw earlier in
the case of the Harry Potter analogy, it is entirely conceivable that we are all born into
a false representation of a reality shaped by our worldview where truth is decided by
those who can manipulate and control the narrative.
    This problem is not new. Plato illustrated it through the Allegory of the Cave.
People chained inside a cave mistake shadows for reality. It comes from Book VII of
The Republic, where he uses a vivid story to explain the human journey of
awakening—how human beings move from illusion to truth. The prisoners are
chained, facing a wall, and only see shadows cast by objects behind them, assuming,
“This is reality.” The allegory teaches that humans can live inside illusion, mistake
consensus for truth, resist awakening, and approach reality only through painful

reflection. The same problem exists in almost every worldview. A belief may appear
coherent and correspond to experience, yet still be false if it is calibrated against the
wrong standard and harms society in the aggregate.
    This aspect is elegantly captured in the film The Truman Show, where the
protagonist is raised inside a massive, simulated world, fully believing his life is real.
But then he begins to suspect something is wrong. He only breaks free after noticing
systematic glitches that cannot be explained by his immediate surroundings. To
resolve these inconsistencies, Truman must formulate a hypothesis with the fewest
assumptions. He could choose to remain trapped by refusing to acknowledge the
anomalies generated by his model—rationalizing that “the universe is just acting
weirdly today”—or he could awaken to the far more elegant realization that his entire
reality is a manufactured set.
    Yet while Plato powerfully illustrates awakening from deception, he leaves
unresolved the problem of sustained orientation—how the seeker continually
distinguishes deeper reality from new, more convincing shadows. Escape from one
cave could be an entry into another with more sophisticated deception.
    Motive is hard to establish and the only way to infer that actors are acting in bad
faith is through the accumulation of circumstantial evidence. No one is going to give
us a signed confession saying, “Yes, we altered the text to benefit ourselves!” This is
perhaps why corroborating evidence can be dismissed in the present even when a
model satisfies the required truth-conditions because the calibrated mind, in a state of
cognitive dissonance, looks for certainty—the fallacy of demanding absolute proof of
intent—when there is none. Treating the absence of absolute proof as a “gotcha” is a
psychological defense mechanism—one that shields inherited bias rather than
advancing truth. Continuous calibration to track reality as it is—updating our lens
through the structured accumulation of evidence—becomes a lifelong commitment.
   The key insight is this:
  We encounter reality through the lens of calibration. Miscalibration can distort
that reality. Continuous evidence-based calibration is required to observe reality as it
is.

The Problem of Self-Authenticating Revelation
    Later in this book I will be comparing texts that claim their source to be divine. A
claim of divine inspiration is still a claim. Saying “God revealed this to me” does not
transform the statement into evidence of its own divine origin. If it did, every person
who claimed revelation would have to be accepted on precisely the same grounds.
    Paul claimed revelation from Jesus based on a vision. Joseph Smith claimed angelic
revelation. Mirza Ghulam Ahmad claimed divine revelation. Ezekiel claimed visions of
God that became accepted as canonical revelation within Judaism. Religious history is
filled with founders, prophets, mystics and sectarian leaders making comparable
claims. Many of their claims contradict one another, so the mere assertion of
revelation cannot establish which, if any, actually came from God. It must be
demonstrated.
   An additional evidentiary foundation is therefore required—something
independent of the claimant's assertion against which the claim can be tested.
    Religious communities routinely reverse this epistemic order. They inherit a body
of literature as part of their accepted epistemic framework and then treat claims
contained within that literature as authenticated because the literature itself is already
considered authoritative. Competing revelatory claims are subjected to skepticism,
while claims embedded within the inherited tradition are considered sacred and
granted presumptive divine status.
   The reasoning becomes circular:
    Inherited epistemic source → claimant is authoritative → claimant says God
revealed X → X is therefore divine → X reinforces the authority of the inherited
epistemic source.
   The evidentiary calibration of the devotee changes depending upon whose
metaphysical claim is being evaluated. What is accepted as evidence is determined by
inherited authoritative calibration.

A Christian may accept Paul's claimed revelation while demanding evidence from
Joseph Smith. A Sunni may accept waḥy ghayr matlūw (“unrecited revelation”)
attributed to Muhammad through later reports and jurisprudential doctrine while
rejecting the revelatory claims of competing sects. Twelver Shiʿism accepts the Imams,
beginning with Ali, as divinely appointed and endowed with authoritative knowledge
unavailable through ordinary human means. All religious belief systems are based on
the assumption that their own epistemic sources of choice have special status over
others, but unless an independent criterion distinguishes these claims, the difference is
not evidentiary. It is inherited calibration.
   The key insight is this:
    Divine authority cannot be established merely by asserting divine authority. To
avoid circular reasoning, the claim requires an evidentiary foundation external to the
claim itself.
   To be fair, every scriptural tradition runs into at least one of the horns of
Agrippa’s Trilemma. Bypassing this epistemic finitude constraint requires an
independent truth-tracking framework. In this book, that framework is CCC:
Coherence, Correspondence and Calibration. A claim must cohere internally and with
established knowledge, correspond with the evidence and lived reality, and survive
comparison against competing explanations under an equally applied calibration.
CCC itself receives no exemption: if a better truth-tracking methodology can be
demonstrated, it should replace it.
   The CCC Framework runs in the following direction:
   Claim → CCC → evidence → comparison with competing hypotheses →
conclusion requiring minimal ad hoc rescues and lowest explanatory friction.
    Not:
   Claim → inherited acceptance → divine authority.
    This book proceeds from the assumption that no claim becomes divine merely by
claiming divine origin. Every claim enters the evidentiary field from unproven first
axioms at the start and must earn its truth value under the same truth-tracking
criteria.

Assumptions and Methodology
   The book begins with the foundational assumption that a Creator exists and has
communicated revelation to humanity. While the CCC framework is applicable to
evaluating any worldview and by itself doesn't force a theocentric assumption at the
start, this specific book narrows its scope to testing the candidate revelatory claims of
the Abrahamic traditions within that framework. It does not assume which surviving
textual tradition, if any, reliably preserves that revelation. Determining that question
is the purpose of this investigation, which is limited to Jewish, Christian and Islamic
textual traditions.
    For a revelation claim to serve as a valid calibration point for tracking reality, the
historical vehicle itself (the text) must first be calibrated and tested for historical and
structural integrity. In other words, recalling our earlier puzzle analogy, instead of
trying to build the puzzle without the lid, we are looking for the best lid that allows us
to create a puzzle whose pieces fit neatly together. Relying on piecing the puzzle
together without the picture on the lid is an extraordinarily slow process, fraught with
false starts, anthropocentric dead ends, and constrained by mortality. Relying on true
revelation as the picture on the lid can dramatically shorten the path to discovering
how reality is structured.
   Our goal then is to analyze the texts and select the one that provides the best
explanatory fit with the fewest assumptions. The inference of divinity has to be
demonstrated, not merely asserted.

   The CCC Framework requires equality in the application of method, not equality
of truth value among competing claims. If one claim survives the criteria and another
does not, treating their conclusions as epistemically equivalent would itself violate
methodological neutrality. The stronger revelatory claim is therefore not the one that
provides impossible absolute justification with no margin for error, but the one that
produces the lowest explanatory friction across coherence and correspondence when
abductively compared across independent evidentiary domains, including authorship,
transmission, preservation, manuscript evidence, textual coherence, historical
correspondence and claims of authority.
   Any serious truth claim must expose itself to potential falsification. A claim of
divinity or a “divinely inspired” text cannot be proven with certainty. It can only be
inferred. Revelation, within the CCC Framework, is therefore treated not as dogma
but as a truth claim open to the same epistemic testing as competing revelatory claims.
The inquiry is consequently epistemic rather than merely theological. Truth claims are
evaluated inferentially on the coherence, correspondence and calibration axes as

opposed to metaphysical claims predicated on faith, private visions or on the ex post
spotless character of individuals.
    Selection by way of the CCC Framework does not guarantee certitude. However,
as independent evidence converges upon the same explanation, rational confidence
can increase. As shown in Figure 1.4, the inferential logic of this study follows a
cumulative-inference model of rational evaluation, in which confidence increases
when independent evidentiary vectors repeatedly converge upon the same
explanation. A text could appear to cohere and correspond with reality locally but
whether it does so across multiple domains can only be discovered once we place all
competing texts side-by-side and ask the following questions:
        •  When rules of logic and inner consistency are applied, which text comes
           out on top?
        •   Across a shared empirical criterion, which text corresponds most with
             claims of authorship, transmission, history, and manuscript evidence?
   The central hypothesis is that a revelatory claim, if true, should withstand
sustained stress-testing against the CCC Framework without requiring an increasing
number of auxiliary assumptions or ad hoc rescue mechanisms. If false, sustained
examination should produce increasing explanatory friction through failed
correspondence, internal contradictions, historical discontinuities, unstable authority
claims, textual anomalies, or additional assumptions required to reconcile contrary
evidence.
    In the court of law, direct access to another person’s mind is normally
impossible. Motive and intent then is usually inferred from circumstantial evidence.
In our analysis, we want to also understand as part of our scriptural evaluation,
when a later claim to divinity was made, who benefited? who gained power? were
human interests involved? does the institutional interest appear repeatedly? After
centuries time lapse, we are unlikely to get a signed confession, but the intelligent
reader when the evidence is laid out clearly, can certainly put two and two together.
   The relevant question is therefore not whether an isolated anomaly can be found
in a textual tradition, but what happens to its explanatory burden as independent
evidence accumulates against claims of a divinely inspired text.

The “Deductive-Dominant” Trap
   The recursive nature of calibration is what truly separates a rigid, dogmatic
framework from a dynamic, truth-seeking methodology. Calibration is not a static,
one-time setting. It must constantly update when better evidence is available—rather
than allowing an individual to get locked into a "deductive-dominant" trap where
existing beliefs protect unexamined assumptions at all costs.
    In reality, when evidence does not go in their favor, humans have a tendency to
resort to using deductive gymnastics to protect inherited assumptions. Humans can
almost always construct some explanation to defend their position. When our
deduction and observation repeatedly produce explanatory friction or anomalies, a
rational mind inclined to truth does not use ad hoc rescues—special pleading,
appeals to faith, or goalpost shifting—to protect the premise; it recalibrates. The
CCC Framework serves as a useful diagnostic to reveal what happens to the
explanatory burden as ad hoc excuses multiply and contrary evidence accumulates.
   To arrive at a superior calibration system that tracks truth is a journey of careful
reasoning, observation, and reflection. For most, it is not given at birth; it must be
earned. A truth-tracking system must also operate along a temporal axis. It must not
only explain present observations but anticipate whether a governing principle will
remain aligned as reality unfolds over time. A superior calibration allows human
beings to choose rightly before history finishes delivering its verdict—even when a
miscalibrated system appears to be “winning” in the short term. This predictive
alignment before full empirical revelation is the essence of wisdom.
    Truth-tracking requires the humility to recalibrate when presented with
superior evidence across independent vectors, rather than remaining trapped in a
deductive loop that uses fallacious reasoning merely to defend unexamined
assumptions. No matter how much evidence is presented, if there is no desire to
recalibrate simply because truth is uncomfortable, conclusions will be determined
based on how data is interpreted through the lens of that fixed calibration. True

   The key insight is this:
    Truth-calibrated reasoning requires patience and courage; it recalibrates when a

Conclusion
    People argue because facts are calibration-dependent. The same observations are
interpreted as facts according to the individual’s truth criterion. Logic then works
backward from that target, selecting arguments or data that advance the chosen
standard. Thus, calibration silently governs both correspondence—what counts as
relevant evidence—and coherence—which inferences appear reasonable. Calibration is
the master key of human reasoning. It dictates what we count as a “fact,” how we
interpret feedback, and whether our logic serves to discover reality or merely shield
our biases from it.
    Since truth cannot be grounded from within a closed human reasoning system,
every calibration ultimately begins from unproven first principles. Within the CCC
Framework, abductive comparison therefore becomes the most rational way to
determine which worldview provides the best explanation of reality, coheres
deductively, and corresponds with human history and lived experience along the
trajectory of time. As established, operating without a true calibration point is like
assembling a puzzle without the lid. This book applies the CCC Framework to
abductively determine which scriptural tradition provides the lid—the reference
image by which the pieces can be assembled. The result is necessarily provisional: a
model of reality that is teleologically the most coherent and correspondent with the
evidence available to us.
   The CCC Framework was not chosen because it favors one worldview or
scriptural tradition over another; it was chosen because coherence, correspondence,
and calibration are the minimum conditions any worldview must satisfy to be
considered true in principle. It is a falsifiable approach to test scriptural truth claims

against the shared calibration criteria, as opposed to relying on individual inherited
calibrations that privilege their own texts and authorities of choice. Rejection is not
evidence. Those who do not accept this framework to settle truth claims must

superiority.
   Truth is not arrived at through assumption, unverified beliefs handed down at
birth, or whatever brings us the least discomfort. Our goal is to apply the calibration
that is consistent and corresponds with evidence at our disposal. Only an honest,

  A fixed calibration that operates within a closed system comes with a cost: it
